Preface
This proceedings volume contains extended abstracts of talks presented at the 18th Symposium on Operations Research held at the University of Cologne, September 1-3, 1993. 
The Symposia on Operations Research are the annual meetings of the Gesellschaft für Mathematik, Ökonometrie und Operations Research (GMÖOR), a scientific society providing a link between research and applications in the areas of applied mathematics, economics and operations research. The broad range of interests and scientific activities covered by GMÖOR and its members was demonstrated by about 250 talks presented at the 18th Symposium. As in recent years, emphasis was placed on optimization and stochastics, this year with a special focus on combinatorial optimization and discrete mathematics. We appreciate that with sections on parallel and distributed computing and on scientific computing also new fields could be integrated into the scope of the GMÖOR. 
This book contains extended abstracts of most of the papers presented at the conference. Long versions and full papers of the talks are expected to appear elsewhere in refereed periodicals. 
The contributions were divided into sixteen sections: (1) Theory of Optimization, (2) Computational Methods of Optimization, (3) Combinatorial Optimization and Discrete Mathematics, (4) Scientific Computing, (5) Decision Theory, (6) Mathematical Economics and Game Theory, (7) Banking, Finance and Insurance, (8) Econometrics, (9) Macroeconomics and Economic Theory, (10) Stochastics, (11) Production and Logistics, (12) System and Control Theory, (13) Routing and Scheduling, (14) Knowledge Based Systems, (15) Information Systems and (16) Parallel and Distributed Computing. 
The conference was opened by the Vice Rector of the University, Professor Speth. Further opening addresses were given by the Vice Mayor of Cologne, Mrs. Canisius, and the chairman of the GMÖOR, Professor Bachem. This year, also the GMÖOR Senior Scientist Prize was awarded during the opening ceremony. The awardee was Professor Krelle, one of the founders of the predecessor organizations of GMÖOR. 
The scientific program consisted of semi-plenary lectures, invited and contributed talks. In the semi-plenary lectures, twelve leading experts gave a one-hour survey on recent developments and most interesting results in their fields. Egon Balas and Joel L. Horowitz started this series with talks on the prize collection TSP and bootstrap methods in applied econometrics. They were followed by Michael Ball who spoke about the design and analysis of reliable networks and by Denis Boyssou with a talk on preference modelling. Rainer Burkard and Bernard Cornet gave talks on the Monge property and exchange economies. Bezalel Gavish surveyed recent results in the area of telecommunication and Jonathan Borwein discussed trends in convex programming. Jan Karel Lenstra spoke about local search methods in job shop scheduling and Robert A. Becker dealt with Ramsey equilibria. The series of semi-plenary talks was closed by David Shmoys and Luk van Wassenhove with talks on the role of linear programming for solving scheduling problems and on recent results in logistics. 
With over 370 participants, about 250 presentations, software demonstrations, press conferences and a lot of other designated actions we were able to show that our field remains active, vital and open for new topics. We would like to thank the authors for presenting their papers and the chairmen of the sections, who helped us in attracting so many distinguished speakers from all over the world. We also thank Dr. Müller of Physica-Verlag for his continuous support and cooperation in publishing this proceedings volume and Mrs. Krupp for her patience and perseverance in preparing its final version. 
The conference was generously sponsored by the Ministerium für Wissenschaft und Forschung des Landes Nordrhein-Westfalen and the Deutsche Forschungsgemeinschaft (DFG), which allowed us to support people from abroad and which made it possible that so many authors from Eastern Europe could attend the Symposium. We are deeply indebted to both institutions. 
Finally we would like to thank our university and the members of our organizational staff without whose enthusiastic and unselfish support this symposium would not have been possible.

The Scheduling Problem in High-Level Synthesis: Extended 0/1 LP Model and Solution Methods
Hans Achatz Universität Passau Lehrstuhl Rechnerstrukturen (Prof. W. Grass) D-94030 Passau, Germany 
As demonstrated by the activity in this area high-level synthesis - often referred to as behavioral synthesis - is becoming an increasingly popular research topic. A main part of high-level synthesis is scheduling where each operation from a data flow graph is assigned to a control step besides several constraints. We have formulated the scheduling problem as a 0/1 LP. For the first time we have extended the model to different execution times and multifunctional function units. To decrease the computation time we have developed a method for constructing new lower bounds with the help of LP (!). The results show that computation time is superior to comparable approaches though our model is able to describe a larger set of problems. 
1. INTRODUCTION
High-level synthesis tools transform a behavioral description in terms of algorithm into a RT-level structural description observing several constraints. In order to produce the data path part of a digital processor the following subtasks have to be solved: scheduling, resource allocation and resource binding. This paper is concerned with the scheduling problem as a first step in the sequence of subtasks. Scheduling is the task of assigning operations to control steps optimizing a certain objective function. Several different methods for scheduling have been published. Most of them are heuristics. 
We have extended the $_ { 0 / 1 }$ formulation of Hwang (1991) and Lee (1989) combined with ideas from Gebotys (1991) to enlarge the set of scheduling problems that can be described in our model. These $0 / 1 \ \mathrm { { L P } }$ approaches are very promising with respect to the set of allowable constraints like chaining, multicycle operations, functional pipelining, mutually exclusive operations and loop folding. They also reported very short computing times to get optimal solutions for benchmark examples. However there are at least two drawbacks of these approaches, which are very important for practical applications. They cannot correctly handle multifunctional function units with different operator combinations and they require assigning operation time to operation instances in the behavior description. In practice operation time is a feature of the function unit (FU) or even better of an operation performed by a module. In the second case, different operations of a module for example "add" and "and" may be done in different execution times. The algorithm to be described has been implemented as a part of a high-level synthesis system SUSAN (System for Universal Scheduling and Allocation) (see Achatz (1993b)) transforming VHDL behavioral description into composition of formally verified RT-level modules.

3. GENERATING LOWER BOUNDS VIA LP 
The efficiency of solving the $0 / 1 \bf { L P }$ is extremely dependent on good bounds. In our approach we generate lower bounds via LP. In all applications so far we did not have problems with computing times. For determination of these lower bounds we have to distinguish between time-constrained and resource-constrained scheduling. In the time-constrained model we assume that the number of csteps is given and that we want to minimize the hardware costs. If we know how many units of one type are needed at least the solution space would be reduced. In order to achieve a lower bound for the number of FU-types needed we construct a new objective function 
minimize 
$$
{ \mathfrak { c } } _ { \mathfrak { k } } \cdot \mathbf { M } _ { \mathfrak { k } }
$$
Notice that the objective function only depends on one type of function unit. The problem is solved as LP (!). Therefore computing times are no problem. The solution expresses how many FUs of Type tk are needed at least if all other FU-types do not have any costs. The optimal value of the cost function can be rounded up because the LP solution is always a lower bound for the $0 / 1 \ \mathrm { \bf L P }$ solution. This estimation can be made for every FU-type. So we have generated lower bounds for every FU-type and therefore reduced the solution space drastically. For our experiments most of the lower bounds were also the optimal values. If there are multi-functional FUs with non disjunctive operation type sets the bounds are of course weaker, because one FUtype can replace the other. In a similar way we can compute new bounds for resource-constrained scheduling.

AN APPLICATION OF AN INTERIOR POINT METHOD FOR PROBLEMS WITH UNCERTAINTY 
Anna Altman \*   
Systems Research Institute,   
Polish Academy of Sciences   
ul. Newelska 6, 01-447 Warszawa,   
Poland 
Interior point methods (IPM) were first proposed for linear programming (LP) problems. Since 1984, when Karmarkar published his famous paper (Karmarkar (1984)), IPM were rapidly developed and improved. It is now widely accepted that the primal-dual logarithmic barrier method is the most efficient IPM. The idea of IPM was not restricted to linear programming alone. These methods quickly spread to quadratic, nonlinear and integer programming. The developments in quadratic programming (QP) are closely parallel to those in LP. While theoretical worst-case behaviour for LP and QP are the same, QP problems are harder to solve in practice. The derivation of the higher order primal-dual method for QP is analogous to the derivation for the linear case presented in Mehrotra (1991) and implemented by Altman and Gondzio (1992). 
Let us consider the QP problem 
$$
{ \begin{array} { r } { \operatorname { m i n i m i z e } c ^ { T } x + { \frac { 1 } { 2 } } x ^ { T } Q x , } \\ { { \mathrm { s u b j e c t ~ t o } } ~ A x = b , } \\ { x + s = u , } \\ { x , s \geq 0 , } \end{array} }
$$
where $\mathbf { \Delta } _ { c } , \mathbf { \Delta } _ { x } , s , u \in \mathcal { R } ^ { n }$ , $b \in \mathcal { R } ^ { m }$ and $A \in \mathcal { R } ^ { m \neq n }$ is assumed to have a full row rank. The dual of (1) is given by 
$$
\begin{array} { r } { \operatorname* { m a x i m i z e } b ^ { T } y - u ^ { T } w - \cfrac { 1 } { 2 } x ^ { T } Q x , } \\ { \mathrm { s u b j e c t ~ t o } ~ A ^ { T } y + z - w - Q x = c , } \\ { x , z , w \geq 0 , } \end{array}
$$
where $\pmb { y } \in \mathcal { R } ^ { m }$ and $\pmb { x } , \pmb { z } , \pmb { w } \in \mathcal { R } ^ { n }$ 
Having incorporated inequality-type constraints in a logarithmic term added to the objective function, we obtain the first order necessary conditions for the optimal solution of (1) and (2): 
$$
\pmb { A x } = \pmb { b } ,
$$
$$
\begin{array} { r } { x + s = u , } \\ { A ^ { T } y + z - w - Q x = c , } \\ { X Z e = \mu e , } \\ { S W e = \mu e , } \\ { x , s , z , w > 0 , } \end{array}
$$
where $x , s , z$ and $W$ are diagonal matrices with the elements $\boldsymbol { x } _ { j } , \boldsymbol { s } _ { j } , \boldsymbol { z } _ { j }$ and ${ \pmb w } _ { j }$ , respectively and $e \in \mathcal { R } ^ { n }$ is a vector of ones. There is only one difference between necessary conditions for LP and QP, namely the term $\boldsymbol { Q x }$ in (5). 
The basic primal-dual algorithm applies one iteration of Newton's method to find an approximate solution to (3-7) for a current (fixed) parameter $\pmb { \mu }$ and terminates when the duality gap is reduced to a predetermined tolerance. It may be viewed as an optimal trajectory from a given starting point to the optimum of (1) and (2). In the Mehrotra method a higher order Taylor approximation of the optimal trajectory is computed. Knowing Taylor terms we can compute the search directions in the primal and dual spaces. 
Undoubtedly, the main computational effort in any IPM implementation is expanded in solving for the direction. To obtain the direction we solve the normal equation with matrix $A ^ { T } \Theta A$ . The difference between linear case and quadratic one is in the matrix $\Theta$ , for the quadratic case 
$$
\Theta = ( Q + X ^ { - 1 } Z + S ^ { - 1 } W ) ^ { - 1 } .
$$
For a linear case $Q = 0$ and other matrices in (8) are diagonal, and computing $\Theta$ is very simple. In many practical problems the matrix $Q$ is either diagonal or we can reformulate the problem (1) to obtain a diagonal quadratic matrix, cf. Vanderbei and Carpenter (1993). Therefore computing $\Theta$ for separable QP is as easy as for a LP. 
The method described above was used for finding cost-effective environmental policy under uncertainty. We considered the problem of reduction of $S O _ { 2 }$ emissions in Europe, cf. Amann et al. (1993). We minimize European-wide emission control costs subject to the condition that grid-specific $s O _ { 2 }$ deposition targets are attained. The costs of emission control are uncertain. There are many possible scenarios of energy production, consumption, fuel characteristics and installed emission control measures. Having scenarios and their probabilities we construct mean-variance model. In such a method we minimize a weighted sum of expected value of total costs and their variance. 
Finally we construct the stochastic optimization problem: 
$$
\begin{array} { r } { \operatorname* { m i n } E \{ c ( x ) \} + \alpha V \{ c ( x ) \} } \\ { \mathrm { ~ s . ~ t . ~ } T x \leq b , } \\ { l \leq x \leq u , } \end{array}
$$
where $\pmb { c } ( \pmb { x } )$ is a random function and costs are values of this function, $\pmb { x }$ denotes emissions to be reduced, $\pmb { b }$ is a vector of maximal grid depositions, and depositions $\pmb { y }$ are linear function of emissions $\left( { \pmb y } = { \pmb T } { \pmb x } \right)$ . 
The costs for each country are piecewise linear convex functions. Since variance is a quadratic function, the objective function is quadratic too. The problem is reformulated in such a way that the quadratic part of the objective is separable. All constraints are linear. 
To make the model more useful for decision makers we solve the model with different weighting factors for the variance. It enables us to control the value of variance at the solution found; in other words, uncertainty or risk. The method was successfully used at 
IIASA in Transboundary Air Pollution Project. We hope that these ideas can be exploited for a wider class of other stochastic optimization problems.
