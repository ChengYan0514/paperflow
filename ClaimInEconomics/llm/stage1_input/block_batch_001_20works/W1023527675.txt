1. Introduction
"Brag-and-moan" is the norm for the Internet feedback systems used to promote trust by a growing number of online and offline markets—for example, Amazon, eBay, Etsy, Taobao, Airbnb, Google reviews, TripAdvisor, and Yelp. Reviews of trade satisfaction tend to extremes: very positive reviews are more prevalent than very negative reviews, with each of these more frequent than moderate reviews (Hu et al. 2009, Filipas et al. 2018). As we will see, the literature finds that feedback tends to be compressed in that trader performance is more disperse than the brag-and-moan pattern implies. Two traders can have quite similar feedback profiles even though one has been a more reliable trading partner. As such, compression can lead to misplaced trust. Understanding the factors behind this compression is an important step toward designing more informative feedback systems. 
In this paper, we investigate whether uncertainty about seller responsibility can lead to lenient feedback giving. We hypothesize that in the face of such uncertainty, people prefer making leniency errors to making severity errors; that is, they avoid giving critical feedback if doing so risks punishing an honest but unlucky trader. Traders do not typically observe all the actions of their trading partners. When problems arise, there can be attributional uncertainty as to the cause; for example, finding that the hot water is not working in a rented vacation flat might be due to owner negligence but also might be due to bad luck beyond the owner's control. Theoretical and experimental evidence, much of it from the labor and law and economics literatures, shows that, in the face of attributional uncertainty, people tend to rate leniently. We hypothesize that this tendency extends to market feedback rating systems. In feedback systems, leniency for problematic performance can take two forms, either as an inflated rating that partially or fully overlooks the problem or as silence, electing not to rate at all. Both forms of leniency would lead to compression; because shortcomings tend not to be reported, less reliable traders can have similar feedback scores to more reliable traders. 
Although there is a good deal of research on the functioning of reputation systems in markets, a general understanding of the behavioral antecedents of feedback provision is missing. There has been but a little research on basic questions such as why and how people give feedback (largely a public good) and how feedback is used to make market decisions.1 Controlled laboratory studies of known field phenomena can help to isolate the missing behavioral mechanisms (Roth 2002). One of the challenges of testing the leniency hypothesis is separating leniency from any response feedback givers might have to exogenous uncertainty per se. For example, if the exogenous shock has a downward bias so that, on average, buyers receive less than sellers send, then traders might adjust their feedback ratings upward simply to account for the expected shortfall; the feedback language is, in effect, adjusted to the expected shipment value with minimal loss of informativeness. 
Exploiting the control the laboratory affords, we designed a market setting with optional feedback in which we can directly manipulate whether the received quality is a true or noisy signal of seller effort. In the Baseline treatment, the level of quality the buyer receives is equal to what the seller shipped. In the Uncertainty treatment, shipments are subject to a random shock; the expected quality of what is received closely approximates the level shipped, but the amount actually received varies. Traders do not observe the shock. Absent a propensity for leniency, we expect possibly noisier feedback but no average differences in silence and feedback ratings across treatments (our null hypothesis). If, however, there is a propensity for leniency, then under Uncertainty, the feedback giving rate should fall and average feedback should rise for cases where quality received falls short of what the buyer was lead to expect. The experiment also admits the reverse bias: a higher level of $" \mathrm { m o a n } ^ { \prime \prime }$ feedback. Although we judged this less likely, it is plausible given earlier findings that punishment for bad outcomes is an important motivator for feedback giving (Lafky 2014). 
Our data support the hypothesis that when attribution is uncertain, buyers tend to give sellers the benefit of the doubt when receiving less than advertised, either by leaving lenient ratings or by choosing not to rate at all. These reporting biases reduce the informativeness of feedback, making it harder for buyers to differentiate between honest and dishonest sellers. Sellers respond strategically, shipping lesser quality under uncertainty. Buyers, however, bid and pay about the same prices in the Baseline and Uncertainty treatments. Overall, the strategic behavior of sellers significantly decreases buyer profits and market efficiency. Implications for market design and reputation studies are discussed in the conclusion section. 
3. Experimental Design
We investigate the effect of uncertainty on market feedback ratings in an auction market. There are two treatments. One introduces a factor that randomly distorts a seller's shipped quality, whereas the other does not. In both treatments, market transactions take place over a series of periods, and in each period, one seller and two buyers play the stage game outlined in Table 1. 
In the first stage, the seller publicly announces a quality $q _ { a }$ from the interval $( 0 \% , 1 0 0 \% )$ . The announcement corresponds to item descriptions sellers typically post on internet market sites and are the basis of buyer expectations for what will be received in a transaction. In addition, the seller privately chooses a quality $q _ { s }$ she is going to ship from the same interval at linear cost $c ( q _ { s } ) = q _ { s }$ . 
In the second stage, buyers learn their valuation $v _ { i } ,$ which is privately drawn from a uniform distribution of all integers between 100 and 300. Buyers also learn the announced quality $q _ { a } ,$ the seller's feedback score, and the number of feedback ratings the seller has received so far (given that the seller already received some feedback ratings). The feedback score is the arithmetic mean of all feedback ratings received until the current period. Buyers then submit their bids, with a minimum bid of 100 ECUs (experimental currency units). 
In the third stage, the buyer who submitted the higher of the two bids wins the auction and learns the received quality $q _ { r }$ .He pays a price $p$ amounting to the second-highest bid plus 1 ECU to the seller. In case both bidders state the same bid, the buyer who entered his bid first wins and pays his bid. If only one bidder submitted a bid, he wins and pays the minimum price of 100 ECUs. The payoff for the winning bidder is his valuation $v _ { i }$ multiplied by the received quality $q _ { r }$ net of the price $p$ (not including feedback costs described below): $\pi _ { w b } = v _ { i } \times ( q _ { r } / 1 0 0 ) - p$ The losing bidder receives a payoff of $\pi _ { l b } = 0$ . The seller's payoff is the price $p$ less the costs for the shipped quality: $\pi _ { s } = p - q _ { s }$ If no bidder submits a bid, the product is not sold, and the seller and both bidders receive a payoff of 0.

In the fourth stage, the winning bidder has the opportunity to leave a feedback rating for the seller on a five-point scale from 1 to 5, where 5 is the highest rating. In the case where a buyer posts a feedback rating, his profit is reduced by 1 ECU. 
In the final stage, the buyer and the seller learn their respective payoffs, and the seller also gets to know the feedback rating (in the case where the buyer submitted one) and the updated feedback score. 
In the Baseline treatment, the received quality is equal to the shipped quality. We change this in the Uncertainty treatment where in $5 0 \%$ of all auctions, the buyers receive the shipped quality plus a random integer drawn from a normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation of 10.5 This shock happens randomly, and neither the seller nor the buyer is informed of whether quality has been changed or not. Hence, buyers cannot infer the sellers' responsibility when receiving something else than promised from the received quality with certainty. The shock provides a noisy signal of what the seller sent that is approximately unbiased, the small discrepancy as a result of the cutoffs implied by the admissible quality domain. Specifically, the bias is highest at the cutoffs: the expected shock to a shipment quality of 0 or 100 is, respectively, $+ 3 . 9 9$ or $- 3 . 9 9$ quality units, whereas the expected shock to a shipment quality of 50 is 0. There is a $7 5 \%$ chance that the buyer will receive at least as much as was shipped and a $9 2 \%$ chance that the shortfall will be no more than 10 units for intermediate values of shipped quality.6 
The stage game was repeated for 45 periods. At the beginning of each period, subjects were randomly assigned the role of a seller or a bidder with the restriction that each subject was in the role of a seller for exactly 15 periods. In each period, one seller was matched with two buyers under the restriction that sellers did not meet the same buyer(s) in two consecutive periods. Each session consisted of 30 participants, which were assigned to five matching groups.' Subjects were rematched with other subjects from their matching group only, such that there are five independent observations per session. Across matching groups, bidder valuations and the matching—including the sequence of roles8—were held constant. At the beginning of the experiment, subjects received an endowment of 1,000 ECUs. Gains and losses were added to or deducted from this initial endowment. 
The structure of the experiment builds on that used by Bolton et al. (2013) with the key differences that there is uncertainty about seller responsibility, only buyers have the opportunity to leave feedback, and sellers make a nonbinding quality announcement at the beginning of each period. The last feature controls for buyer expectations. 
We ran two sessions per treatment with 120 participants in total. The four sessions were run in the Cologne Laboratory for Economic Research (CLER) at the University of Cologne in November and December 2012. Subjects were recruited from the CLER's subject pool using ORSEE (Online Recruitment System for Economic Experiments; Greiner 2004), and the computerized experiment was run using $\mathbf { Z }$ Tree (Fischbacher 2007). At the end of the experiment, the account balance was converted to euros $( 1 0 0 \mathrm { E C U s } =$ €1) and paid out in cash. On average, subjects earned $\in 2 1 . 2 8 ,$ and sessions lasted for approximately two hours. 
3.1. Hypotheses
As explained above, the exogenous shock is designed so that, absent a bias in feedback behavior, the introduction of uncertainty should not bias expected feedback scores. Specifically, whereas the introduction of uncertainty might lead to more noise in feedback giving, we take as our null hypothesis that average feedback given, along with the frequency of feedback giving, by a buyer who receives $q _ { r }$ when the seller announced quality $q _ { a }$ is the same across Baseline and Uncertainty treatments. Taking $q _ { r } / q _ { a }$ as the measure of seller honesty, the fill ratio, we write the null hypothesis as follows. 
Hypothesis 0. Changes to feedback giving from the introduction of uncertainty largely reflect the added noise: the variance for feedback for $q _ { r } / q _ { a } < 1$ may rise, but the average feedback and the frequency of feedback giving conditional on the fill ratio are unchanged. 
In line with the discussion in Section 2, the alternative hypothesis anticipates that with the introduction of uncertainty, a benefit of the doubt will exist in the form of higher ratings or lower reporting in cases where buyers have a negative experience. 
Hypothesis 1. Changes to feedback giving from the introduction of uncertainty reflect leniency: the average feedback given for $q _ { r } / q _ { a } < 1$ will be greater than in the Baseline treatment, or there is a reduced frequency of feedback given. 
Hypothesis 1 implies that in the Uncertainty treatment, feedback scores will become compressed in the sense that sellers who are very trustworthy and sellers that are less trustworthy will have more similar ratings than in the Baseline treatment. So leniency at the individual level lowers the informativeness of the feedback system in the sense that highly rated sellers' trustworthiness is lower and exhibits higher variance. 
Hypothesis 2. In the Uncertainty treatment, feedback informativeness will be lower as a result of compression in ratings. 
How might leniency affect market outcomes? Lower informativeness gives rise to moral wiggle room in the sense that it creates pecuniary incentives for opportunistic seller behavior. Even sellers that are less strategic minded and more honest might be tempted because, in the present case, uncertainty about attribution makes one's actions less informative about one's true nature and so less damaging to one's self-image (Dana et al. 2007, Bénabou and Tirole 2011, and Ockenfels and Werner 2012; see related results in the ultimatum games of Mitzkewitz and Nagel 1993 and Güth et al. 1996). We would then expect that, in the Uncertainty treatment, the lower expected value of the goods received, along with the greater variance (and so greater risk), would lead buyers to bid lower and less often, resulting in lower prices and lower sales volume. 
Hypothesis 3. In the Uncertainty treatment, sellers are more likely to send lower quality than announced. Buyers bid lower and pay lower prices.

4.2. A Shift in Feedback
Our main hypothesis is that for instances in which the quality received is lower than the quality announced, buyer feedback ratings will be more lenient in the Uncertainty treatment than in the Baseline treatment. Figure 3 compares the average feedback rating across Baseline and Uncertainty treatments at different buyer fill ratios. Figure 3 also shows the share of silent transactions in which buyers did not leave a feedback rating. Also below, Table 3 reports the corresponding average feedback ratings and rates of silence by fill category. 
Looking first at the feedback given: from Figure 3, the average feedback submitted for Overfill and Fulfill is similar between the two treatments. Buyers who receive as much as or more than promised submit average ratings of 4.44.6 in both treatments. Likewise, buyers who receive much less than announced, as represented by the Vshortfill classification, usually give the lowest possible rating in both treatments. For the 
Shortfill categories, however, buyers tend to leave higher ratings in the Uncertainty treatment compared with the Baseline treatment, an average of 3.0 versus 2.5. These observations are statistically supported as reported in Table 3. The random effects Tobit estimates in Table 4 control for a number of additional factors, but nevertheless, the same results hold. 
Turning now to the frequency of feedback silence: the overall rate of feedback giving in Baseline and Uncertainty treatments is $6 8 \%$ and $5 7 \%$ ,respectively. By way of comparison, the provision of one-sided (conventional) feedback on eBay is about $7 0 \%$ (Bolton et al. 2013) and about $6 8 \%$ in an online labor marketplace studied by Filipas et al. (2018). Figure 3 shows the frequency of silence in each treatment, broken down by the received-to-announced fill ratio. There is little difference in silence across treatments for the Fulfill category, where buyers receive as much as promised. By contrast, buyers who receive more (Overfill) or less than promised (Shortfill and Vshortfill) remain silent more often in the Uncertainty treatment. Hence, positive and negative surprises are reported less frequently. The statistical analysis reported in Table 3 confirms these observations. The random effects probit models reported in Table 5 tell a similar story. 
Our results are in line with Hypothesis 1. Buyers give more lenient feedback to sellers when what is received is less than announced and responsibility is unclear. In particular, if the received shortfall is less than $2 0 \%$ below what was announced, we observe leniency. The second part of Hypothesis 1 is also confirmed. Silence is more frequent in the Uncertainty treatment in the case of Shortfill than in the case of Fulfill. 
Interestingly, the greater silence is also true for Overfills in the Uncertainty treatment. The leniency hypothesis does not anticipate greater silence on the part of satisfied buyers, but we see it: in both categories where small and relatively probable shocks are a potential explanation for not receiving the expected amount, Shortfills and Overfills, buyers remain silent in $5 1 \%$ of the cases, higher than in Vshortfills, where the silence rate is $2 4 \% ,$ or Fulfills, where the rate is $4 1 \%$ . So it appears that the tendency to silence not only exists for small negative surprises but also extends to small positive surprises: buyers are reluctant to reward, as well as punish, sellers when there is attributional uncertainty. Yet silence for positive surprises also contributes to the feedback compression in our data. Note from Figure 2 that a meaningful number of shipments are Overfills. As many of the overfilling sellers are not rewarded appropriately, they tend to look more similar to sellers who ship less and are not punished. (Also see the discussion of Overfills in Section 4.4.)

4.3. Effect of Lenient Rating Behavior on Seller Feedback Scores
In this section, we examine the effect of lenient rating behavior in the Uncertainty treatment on the distribution of the seller feedback scores. We cannot directly compare the feedback scores between treatments, as these scores are associated with different underlying seller honesty distributions (see Figures 1 and 2). A fair comparison needs to correct for this difference between treatments. (For completeness, the comparison of the unconditional feedback score distributions can be found in Figure 1 in Online Appendix B.) The preceding section showed differences in feedback giving between the treatments, in terms of both tendencies to silence and tendencies how to rate the shipment. It is not directly obvious how to quantify the size of the biases introduced by the inflation and omissions and their effect on feedback scores in the Uncertainty treatment. 
To approximate the overalleffect the feedback giving biases exert on the feedback scores, we estimate regressions of feedback giving in the Baseline data and then predict feedback giving in the Uncertainty treatment using the Baseline regression models. The aim is to get a counterfactual estimate of seller feedback scores for the Uncertainty treatment with predicted Baseline treatment feedback giving. We run two regressions for the Baseline data: a probit model for the decision whether to leave a rating and a Tobit model for the actual rating. For each successful auction in the Uncertainty treatment, using the obtained regression estimates, we first predict whether a rating is left and, if yes, the rating itself. The seller's feedback score is updated, and the same calculation is done for the next transaction. The details of this procedure are described in Online Appendix C. 
The top left panel of Figure 4 contains kernel density estimates for actual and predicted seller feedback scores in the Uncertainty treatment. Compared with the actual data, the distribution of the predicted feedback scores is shifted to the left, implying that actual aggregated feedback scores in the Uncertainty treatment are inflated relative to the Baseline feedback scores. To assess the fit of the predictions, the top right panel shows actual and predicted feedback scores for the Baseline treatment itself. Predicted feedback scores track the actual scores relatively well, although very high scores are slightly overrepresented and very low scores are slightly underrepresented. 
The two panels on the bottom give the distributions of actual and predicted feedback ratings in both treatments and allow us to observe compression introduced by uncertainty. In the Uncertainty treatment, feedback ratings of 2 occur relatively more often in the prediction than in the actual data, driving the downward trend in feedback scores seen in the top left panel. In summary, when using feedback giving in the Baseline treatment as a benchmark, seller feedback scores in the Uncertainty treatment appear inflated. Because of the differences in feedback giving across our treatments, sellers who do not deserve a certain feedback score are now bunched with sellers who do. In the following sections, we discuss whether this leads to a reduced informativeness of the feedback system. 
4.4. Predictiveness of the Feedback System
Perhaps the most important function of the feedback system is to distinguish honest sellers, who ship at least the level of quality they have announced, from sellers who ship less than announced. The informativeness of a feedback system is higher if it discriminates better between honest and dishonest sellers. To compare how well feedback information predicts seller honesty across treatments, we ran a probit regression for each treatment, the dependent variable indicating whether the seller was honest or not,13 regressed on all the information that is available to the buyer before bidding: the seller's feedback score, the number of feedback ratings the seller has received so far, the announced quality, and the current period (see Table 1 in Online Appendix A for the regressions). From these two regressions, we calculate the predicted probabilities to encounter an honest seller along all feedback averages for the two treatments. The results appear in Figure 5. 
Observe that the expected probability of meeting an honest seller is lower for all feedback scores when there is uncertainty about seller responsibility. In particular, the predicted probability of meeting an honest seller is much lower—and noisier—for high feedback scores in the Uncertainty treatment. For example, a feedback score of 4 implies an $8 7 \%$ chance (confidence interval of $8 1 \% - 9 4 \%$ of receiving at least as much as announced in the Baseline treatment, but it declines to a $5 9 \%$ chance $( 4 4 \% - 7 7 \% )$ in the Uncertainty treatment. A similar picture arises when looking at the best possible feedback score of 5, where chances of honest quality still differ by 17 percentage points: $9 7 \%$ $( 9 4 \% - 1 0 0 \% )$ versus $8 0 \%$ $( 6 7 \% - 9 4 \%$ . Another way of looking at the informativeness of the feedback system is to compare receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, which measure the discriminatory performance of the model (Hanley and McNeil 1982, Cleves 2002). The area under the ROC curve in the probit regression for the Baseline (Uncertainty) treatment is 0.85 (0.803). The hypothesis that these areas are equal can be weakly rejected; $p =$ 0.066. Similarly, the Brier score is lower in the Baseline model compared with the Uncertainty treatment (0.133 versus 0.173). So predictions from feedback in the uncertainty system are less able to differentiate honest sellers from opportunistic sellers, which is evidence for Hypothesis 2. 
Recall that the null hypothesis for our experiment is that the introduction of uncertainty leads to perhaps noisier feedback but not biased feedback. An important question then is the extent to which our results can be explained by the introduction of uncertainty per se, without reference to the bias in feedback giving. To examine the predictiveness of the feedback system when feedback giving is noisy but not biased, we ran a Monte Carlo simulation with 1,000 iterations of noisy feedback giving in the Baseline treatment.14 We mapped the uncertainty on the quality scale to the feedback scale by adding draws from a normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation of 0.5 to each actual feedback rating submitted in the Baseline treatment.15 From these new ratings, we recalculated sellers' feedback scores and predicted their honesty using the same model as in Figure 5. The left panel of Figure 6 shows that neither the mean prediction nor the confidence interval change noticeably. Unrealistically large levels of noise are needed to increase the confidence interval and flatten the curve until feedback scores become uninformative (see Figure 3 in Online Appendix B). Noisy feedback giving alone does not lead to the drop in predicted seller honesty in the Uncertainty treatment. 
On the other hand, simulated lenient feedback giving shifts the curve downward: the right panel of Figure 6 shows the results of a Monte Carlo simulation in which each actual feedback rating in the Baseline treatment was increased by 1 (simulations with alternative increase values appear in Figure 4 in Online Appendix B). Predicted seller honesty for low-to-medium feedback scores decreases to a level comparable to what we observe in the Uncertainty treatment, whereas the decrease is not as pronounced for sellers with very high feedback scores. This can be accounted for by the difference in seller honesty: in the Uncertainty treatment, sellers are less likely to ship what they announce. In the Baseline treatment, sellers with high but not perfect feedback scores are more honest such that the simulated leniency does not affect the prediction to a large extent. Hence, a combination of lenient feedback giving and the decrease in seller honesty shown in Section 4.1 can help explaining the drop in the prediction of seller honesty seen in Figure 5, whereas the exogenous shock on quality alone cannot. That is, adding noise to the system alone does not decrease predictiveness, but lenient feedback giving on the buyer side and a change in seller behavior does. 
Figure 7 provides an alternative way of looking at the information in Figure 5. Here, we can see the deviations from shipping the announced quality, given a seller's feedback score. Observe that sellers with high feedback scores in the Uncertainty treatment are more likely to Shortfill than those in the Baseline treatment. 
Figure 7 also provides insight into the nature of Overfill. We might have thought that those sellers most likely to overfill would be those with the highest feedback scores. In fact, the figure shows that overfilling is most prevalent among those with mediocre feedback scores. Plausibly, these sellers are overfilling in an attempt to curry favor with buyers and improve their feedback scores. In fact, as shown in Table 3, the rating frequency for the Baseline treatment is higher for Overfili than Fulfill, such that in this treatment, overfilling increases the probability of getting a high feedback score. Conversely, this strategy does not work in the Uncertainty treatment because of the high silence rate for Overfills in this treatment. 
4.5. Buyer Use of the Feedback System
The preceding discussion on the predictiveness of the feedback score has shown that, conditional on the score, seller honesty decreases and the precision of this estimate becomes smaller as a function of lenient feedback giving and more dishonest seller behavior. Besides the predictiveness of the feedback scores, another important measure of the informational content of a feedback system is how well buyers use the information provided to form expectations about seller behavior. Table 6 offers three such measures: the squared and absolute prediction errors (shipped quality minus expected quality) and the percentage of buyers who expect more than what was actually shipped. We define expected quality as a buyer's bid divided by his valuation. 
Whereas both squared and absolute prediction errors are larger in the Uncertainty treatment than in the Baseline treatment, the differences are not significant. These two measures, however, do not distinguish between overly optimistic and overly pessimistic predictions. In the Uncertainty treatment, $3 4 \%$ of subjects expected more than they received $\%$ Overexpectation), which is significantly higher than the $2 1 \%$ of subjects in the Baseline treatment. Hence, buyers do not make worse predictions in general in the Uncertainty treatment, but their expectations are too high more often. That is, relative to the Baseline treatment, buyers in the Uncertainty treatment do not fully adjust to the diminished informativeness of the feedback system, even though they experience a higher degree of disappointment as measured by the $\%$ Overexpectation variable. 
To further investigate how buyers form expectations we ran panel Tobit regressions on expected quality and separately interact feedback score and announced quality with a treatment dummy (see Table 2 in Online Appendix A).16 As one would expect, a better feedback score and higher-quality announcement significantly increase buyers' expectations in both treatments. However, the interaction effects with Uncertainty are not significant, and thus we find no treatment differences of how buyers use the available information. The fact that a seller's feedback score has no different effect in Uncertainty treatment indicates that buyers fail to account for lenient ratings when interpreting feedback scores before submitting their bids. Consistent with this, we observe that prices are similar across treatments. On average, the final price is 128 ECUs in the Baseline treatment and 123 ECUs in the Uncertainty treatment.1 Panel Tobit regressions show that the seller's feedback score and the announced quality have a significant positive effect on the final price (see Model 1 in Table 3 in Online Appendix A) but do not indicate a treatment difference. Again, interaction effects in Models 2 and 3 with Uncertainty are not significant and thus do not suggest that feedback score or announcement are interpreted differently across treatments, contrary to the buyer part of Hypothesis 3.

It is interesting to see that sellers display a high level of Shortfill already within the first five periods in the Uncertainty treatment (Figure 8). All this said, uncertainty per se may play a role of the shift in seller fulfillment behavior observed in the Uncertainty treatment. Uncertainty about attribution has been shown to open up moral wiggle room that dampens down the self-image and social image factors that otherwise tend to promote honesty and generosity (Dana et al. 2006, 2007; Andreoni and Bernheim 2009; Tadelis 2011). With noise, the buyers in our experiment are unsure about attribution, potentially reducing the detrimental effect of Shortfills on seller social image. Less lenient, more stringent feedback would punish this kind of behavior, with lower feedback scores leading to lower future profits. So in this sense, even though it is uncertainty that creates moral wiggle room, leniency plays a role by not punishing the lapse in trustworthy behavior. 
4.7. Sellers' Reaction to Feedback
To closer investigate the disciplining effect of the feedback system, we analyze sellers' reaction to feedback ratings. We test whether the likelihood that a short filling seller becomes honest in the next period depends on the change in his feedback score as a result of the current feedback rating and whether this reaction is different between treatments. This also speaks to the causes of the changes in seller behavior across treatments. Although uncertainty itself certainly plays a role, we will see that in the Uncertainty treatment, the sellers do not react to the feedback as much as they do in the Baseline treatment. The two models in Table 8 show the results of random effects probit regressions with a seller's trustworthiness $0 =$ dishonest and $1 =$ honest) in the next period as the dependent variable. The models are restricted to sellers who ship less than announced in the current period. To measure the effect of feedback ratings, we use the variable Change in feedback score, which is the difference between the received feedback rating and the current feedback score. Thus, a positive (negative) value of the continuous variable Change in feedback score indicates that the received feedback was above (below) the seller's current feedback score, and thus the score increases (decreases) in the following period. 
Model 1 shows that feedback ratings below the current feedback score significantly increase the likelihood that a dishonest seller becomes honest in the following period. The larger this difference, the higher the probability that the seller changes his behavior. The effects of uncertainty in both models confirm what we saw in Figure 8: in the Uncertainty treatment, sellers in general are less likely to become more trustworthy. Furthermore, the interaction effect in Model 2 between the treatment dummy and the decrease in feedback score shows that the disciplining effect of bad feedback ratings is significantly lower in the Uncertainty treatment. The marginal effect of a change in a seller's feedback score in the Baseline treatment is $- 0 . 1 1 8$ $( p { \le } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and $- 0 . 0 2 3$ $( p =$ 0.224) in the Uncertainty treatment. This means that sellers react less to bad feedback in the Uncertainty treatment. A possible explanation could be that they expect that an unlucky draw of the distortion factor also provides an excuse for a low(er) feedback score so that buyers give the benefit of the doubt and assume sellers to be trustworthy despite a negative signal. 
4.8. Does Honesty Pay?
As shown above, sellers falling short are more likely to get away with no negative feedback in the Uncertainty treatment, changing the incentives for honest behavior. In this regard, Model 1 of Table 9 shows that, in aggregate, being honest has a positive effect on seller profits. However, when we allow honest behavior to have different effects in the two treatments (Model 2), we observe that honest behavior pays significantly only in the Baseline treatment. 
Looking at the marginal effects in Model 2, average profits of a previously deceiving seller in the Baseline treatment are 38 ECUs, whereas a previously honest seller in the same treatment earns 50.5 ECUs. This is a significant increase of more than $3 0 \%$ .By contrast, this honesty premium is not present in the Uncertainty treatment: earnings nonsignificantly decrease by $2 . 7 \%$ from 47.3 to 46 ECUs. Overall, these results indicate that uncertainty about seller responsibility hampers the functioning of the feedback system and removes incentives for truthful seller behavior.

5. Discussion and Conclusion
We reported evidence on the influence of seller attributional uncertainty on the performance of a market that relies on a feedback system to prevent seller moral hazard and adverse selection. We find that, with attributional uncertainty, buyers exhibit a preference for making leniency errors over severity errors, giving sellers who provide value moderately less than advertised higher ratings or remaining silent about their performance more frequently than they do when seller attribution is certain. The compression of feedback scores introduced by leniency on the individual level then works its way up the information chain in the reputation system, hampering the predictiveness of sellers' feedback profiles. In the Uncertainty treatment, a buyer is less likely to encounter an honest seller, even if the seller has a perfect feedback score. Hence, feedback profiles do a poorer job in helping buyers to discriminate between seller types. With the increase in moral wiggle room as a result of noise and lenient feedback giving, sellers deliver less value in the Uncertainty treatment. Buyers fail to account for this reduction in the sense that the prices they pay a seller with a given feedback profile are about the same as under the more accurate feedback obtained when seller attribution is certain. As a result, buyers pay most of the cost of seller malfeasance in the Uncertainty treatment.

Overall, seller trustworthiness is significantly lower in the Uncertainty treatment as the number of sellers shipping at least as much as promised declines by over $5 0 \%$ from $7 0 . 3 \%$ to $3 4 . 3 \%$ .Sellers who receive bad ratings are less likely to change their behavior, suggesting that they anticipate that the uncertainty will provide a credible excuse for lower feedback scores. From the standpoint of seller profits, the increase in deceptive behavior is rational. In the Baseline treatment, honest behavior of a previously dishonest seller leads to a significant increase of $3 3 \%$ in a seller's profits in the following period. However, this is no longer the case when uncertainty disguises responsibility. Here, the honesty premium vanishes and nextperiod profits insignificantly decrease by $2 . 7 \%$ . In short, the reputation system based on compressed ratings does not provide sufficient incentives for trustworthy seller behavior. 
Turning to buyer behavior, the fraction of buyers who expected more than they received increases from $2 0 \%$ in the Baseline treatment to $3 4 \%$ in the Uncertainty treatment. This overoptimism leads to prices changing little across treatments. As a result, buyer profits fall $3 5 \%$ in the Uncertainty treatment. Why buyers do not learn to adjust to the less informative nature of the feedback system in the Uncertainty treatment (in contrast to sellers' considerable adjustment) is not clear. One potential explanation is the higher variability associated with using feedback in the Uncertainty treatment to forecast seller reliability (Figure 5). There is a large literature to show that variability in payoff feedback impedes learning about optimal actions (e.g., Bereby-Meyer and Roth 2006, Ockenfels and Selten 2014). Experiments on senderreceiver games also have shown that receivers put more trust than warranted into senders' messages (e.g., Cai and Wang 2006, de Haan et al. 2015), and this tendency might also contribute to slower learning. 
Laboratory experiments allow for careful control of confounding and unobservable variables and, as such, are a valuable complement to other forms of research (see, e.g., Falk and Heckman 2009). However, they are always an abstraction of the many real-world cases they are intended to apply to. Nonetheless, our markets in the laboratory replicate important regularities found in field studies on markets with reputation information. Besides the match of the feedback giving rate and the informational content of silent transactions (see Section 4.2), the effect of reputational information on market outcomes is qualitatively similar in our experiment. Sellers' feedback scores correlate positively with the probability of a sale as well as the price (see Table 6 in Online Appendix A). This basic result has been found in most of the field studies summarized in Bajari and Hortaçsu (2004) and Tadelis (2016) (see also the references cited in Endnote 2). Unlike the field studies, given that we elicited the cost of sellers and the valuation of buyers, we can also speak directly to the effect of reputation information on profits. In both treatments, buyer period profits increase in the sellers' feedback scores. As expected, sellers' period profits do not increase in their feedback score, because sellers with larger feedback scores also tend to ship more, which increases costs. However, the future seller profits in the remaining periods increase in feedback score, such that both sellers and buyers in our experimental markets get value out of the reputational information. 
There is reason to believe that our results underestimate the true magnitude of feedback compression in field marketplaces. Participants in our experiment did not receive any personal information about each other and had no means of communicating. But in a typical transaction, the trading partners know something about one another's identity, perhaps through email exchange. Such social communication can lead to a feeling of empathy (Andreoni and Rao 2011), obligation (Malmendier and Schmidt 2017), or social pressure (Malmendier et al. 2014). The reduction in social distance can increase other-regarding behavior (Hoffman et al. 1996). Hence, it is conceivable that in real-world interactions, the reporting rate of negative experiences is further decreased by social communication and closeness. There might even be a cross effect with uncertainty: fear of punishment for negative feedback that later turns out to be incorrectly founded. 21 A recent study of hotel reviews has shown that when hotels have the opportunity to publicly reply to feedback ratings, the frequency of negative ratings decreases (Proserpio and Zervas 2017). 
The causes of the different forms of leniency, inflated ratings and silence, are not well understood. One conjecture would be that buyers are heterogeneous regarding their cognitive costs of feedback giving. Uncertainty increases these rating costs as the cognitive processes are more involved, causing some buyers who would have left a rating without uncertainty about attribution to remain silent. Those with larger thresholds regarding the cost of feedback giving might differ in their costs of making leniency and severity errors. 
We need more work to identify the boundaries of the influence caused by leniency. It would be interesting to see whether leniency would be observed in a setting where the quality is binary instead of continuous, and sellers can exert effort to increase the probability of high quality. It is possible that leniency plays a lesser role in such a marketplace because the grey area between high and low quality that opens up the room for attributional uncertainty is missing in such a setup. Another open question relates to the interpretation of feedback scores. It is largely unknown how buyers assess feedback scores, how they use them to form expectations about quality, and how this affects their bids. Modeling the dynamics of this system would help our understanding of these issues. 
We see four managerial implications for market design. First is that feedback system performance can be improved by reducing uncertainty about trader attribution in problematic trades. Looking at eBay, we observe actions to reduce uncertainty about seller responsibility. For example, shipping labels for parcels can be directly purchased via eBay and buyers automatically receive tracking numbers. Even so, there are limits to what can be achieved: although shipping labels reduce uncertainty about the source of delay, they do not help to clarify whether damages occurred before or during shipping. In a similar vein, eBay recently increased the number of images that can be included for free in a product listing. However, for items classified as "used" the degree of signs of usage is still subject to interpretation. These examples illustrate how difficult it is to fully eliminate uncertainty about seller responsibility. 
A second approach would be to give greater guidance on what the trader is rating—specifically, to nudge the trader to ignore the attribution issue and just rate what was received in relation to what was announced. Individual feedback scores would still reflect the noise in the system, but lucky draws would cancel out unlucky ones, with the resulting reputations being less compressed and more informative than would be the case with leniency. (See Samak 2013 for some ideas on how to handle the problem of rating over heterogeneous good categories.) 
A third approach would be to put more weight on traders who are less likely to exhibit leniency in the Uncertainty treatment. To explore the potential of this approach, we went to our data and calculated for each subject the average rating for cases where it was shortfilled (not all subjects received qualities in the Shortfill range during the experiment, and of those who did, not all chose to leave feedback, so the following analysis is based on $5 5 \%$ of subjects in Baseline treatment and $7 2 \%$ of subjects in the Uncertainty treatment). The boxplots in Figure 9 summarize the distributions of these averages. The average ratings in the Uncertainty treatment are higher and more spread out. However, there is still a sizeable portion of buyers in the Uncertainty treatment who rate Shortfills similarly "hard" as do raters in the Baseline treatment; specifically, $3 0 . 2 \%$ of the buyers in the Uncertainty treatment shown in Figure 9 on average leave ratings below 2.42, the mean of ratings in the Shortfill category in the Baseline treatment. Hence, one way to fix the compression of ratings would be to identify those buyers who are less prone to the leniency bias and place more weight on their ratings. These data also reinforce an earlier recommendation for improving these systems: making efforts to get silent traders to report feedback (Dellarocas and Wood 2008). The "harder" raters under the Uncertainty treatment tend to be silent more often than their lenient counterparts $( 4 8 \%$ versus $3 0 . 8 \%$ ). We caution that these conclusions are based on rather small sample size; a more complete study needs to be performed. 
Fourth, our results show that silence signals modest unhappiness with a transaction, reinforcing recommendations made in the previous literature to, for example, make available the number of silent transactions on a seller's feedback profile (Dellarocas and Wood 2008) or sorting the search results by a weighted feedback score that also includes silent transactions (Nosko and Tadelis 2015). 
With regard to the broader implications for reputation systems, our findings regarding rating behavior in the Uncertainty treatment may be relevant for credence goods markets where agents also have the possibility to exploit an informational advantage. Credence goods, such as medical treatment, car repair service, or legal advice, are characterized by the fact that after the transaction or consumption, a consumer can assess the derived utility from the good but still does not know whether the good or service provided by the agent was an adequate and efficient choice to solve the consumer's initial problem. Hence, there is uncertainty about the responsibility of the trading partner, and consumers face a similar problem as in our setup. Studies of the influence of reputation systems on moral hazard in credence goods markets show that reputational information may have deterrent effects on an agent's fraudulent behavior (Grosskopf and Sarin 2010, Dulleck et al. 2011, Mimra et al. 2016). In these studies, reputational information is provided by repeated interactions or as an exact history of agents' past actions and not by voluntary and subjective feedback ratings submitted by consumers themselves. However, regarding medical aid, there are specific websites such as Healthgrades.com or RateMyMD.com gathering subjective ratings by consumers about their experiences with doctors. For lawyers and car mechanics, similar internet services now exist. A promising avenue for future research might be to investigate how subjective feedback works in general in credence goods markets and whether the inherent uncertainty also leads to leniency in feedback giving. 
Our results are also related to the growing experimental literature on imperfect public monitoring in repeated games surveyed in Dal Bó and Fréchette (2018). This literature analyzes the cooperation rates and subjects' strategies in prisoner's dilemmas when past actions can only be observed with noise (see Ambrus and Greiner 2012 for related results in public goods games). The main results, which parallel some of ours, are that when monitoring is imperfect, cooperation can still be maintained, but strategies become more forgiving and lenient. The focus of our study, however, is not the cooperation decision after observing a bad signal but rather leniency in the signal provision itself. In our setup, feedback is provided endogenously: after receiving a noisy signal of the quality sent by the seller, buyers decide whether and how to leave feedback. Moreover, we are interested in feedback giving in a market context. Accordingly, our setup includes a market pricing mechanism allowing the terms of trade (cooperation) to fluctuate. Higher prices can be an important incentive for sellers to be trustworthy, and we study whether leniency influences this incentive. 
Finally, a common observation in the theoretical literature of reputation building (such as those referenced in the introduction) is that reputation is just as effective at promoting cooperation between matched pairs who interact repeatedly as it is for strangers who interact just once, so long as the available information about past cooperation is equivalent. In the field, however, the reputation information available to stranger pairs is widely third party in nature, with attributional uncertainty likewise a commonplace. Given this, our findings suggest that institutions that rely on matched pairs to facilitate cooperation are likely to be more effective at facilitating cooperation than otherwise equivalent institutions that rely on stranger pairs, because cooperating in the latter circumstance is more prone to be trust misplaced in an inflated reputation.
