In 1980, Zimbabwe rapidly expanded access to secondary schools, providing a natural experiment to estimate the impact of increased maternal secondary education on child mortality. Exploiting age specific exposure to these reforms, we find that children born to mothers most likely to have benefited from the policies were about $2 1 \%$ less likely to die than children born to slightly older mothers. We also find that increased education leads to delayed age at marriage, sexual debut, and first birth and that increased education leads to better economic opportunities for women. We find little evidence supporting other channels through which increased education might affect child mortality. Expanding access to secondary schools may greatly accelerate declines in child mortality in the developing world today.

1. Introduction
Across the world, studies have generally found that more educated people live longer and healthier lives, and they give birth to fewer but healthier children (Grossman, 2006). However, only a few of these studies have been able to identify causal evidence of the impact of education, and very few studies have been conducted in the developing world (Lochner, 2011). In addition, very few studies have been able to investigate mechanisms through which improved education might affect health outcomes. Given the disproportionate share of the burden of disease as well as persistently high child mortality and fertility rates in developing countries, there is a need to better understand what role education might play in improving development outcomes today. 
The major challenge to better understanding these relationships has been identifying situations in which education has increased in a way that is unrelated to other factors that might also affect health. The most successful studies have looked at natural experiments, usually changes in compulsory schooling laws or age-at-entry policies, to identify plausibly exogenous changes in education. Generally, most of these studies have found that increased levels of education are associated with lower (own) mortality, improved healthy behaviors (including smoking), lower fertility, and lower child mortality; however, not all of the studies come to the same conclusions1. Although evidence is sparse, there is also some evidence that educating mothers might lead to different outcomes than educating fathers, and that higher levels of schooling may be more important than primary schooling (Lochner, 2011). 
In 1980, following independence, Zimbabwe rapidly expanded access to secondary schools for black Zimbabweans, which provides an opportunity to estimate the impact of increased maternal secondary education on child health and fertility outcomes in a setting with both high child mortality and high fertility2. Using age-specific exposure to a set of policies that greatly increased secondary school opportunities for some women, we first estimate the impact of increased maternal education on child survival3. The magnitude of our main result is large: children born to mothers who attended some secondary schooling are 12 percentage points less likely to die, and an additional year of schooling decreases the probability of a child dying by about $2 1 \%$ . We find that the effects are much stronger in rural areas and among less wealthy mothers. 
We next examine important pathways that might explain the relationship between maternal education and child survival. Since the data from the DHS are quite detailed, we examine mechanisms that encompass the broad categories of fertility (age at marriage, first birth, etc.), economic outcomes (household asset ownership, spousal quality, etc.), health service utilization (institutional delivery and vaccinations) and female empowerment (control over household finances and attitudes toward domestic violence). These outcomes are related to the classic theories of how education may improve health directly via allocative efficiency or productive efficiency mechanisms (Grossman, 2006) or indirectly through some of the other benefits of education such as increased income. For example, a mother’s education may directly affect a child’s health outcomes if it influences the likelihood that she completes all required pre-natal visits, she fully vaccinates her children, or she makes more informed choices with regards to healthcare providers. Alternatively, a more educated mother may have more economic opportunities and she may use these to gain access to more or better quality health care providers. As in McCrary and Royer (2011), we cannot distinguish between direct and indirect channels via which maternal education might affect child health. The health services provided to children of women were examined since it is believed that mothers have a great deal of discretion over the services that their children receive and because timely and more adherent treatment has been linked to improved child health outcomes. Across these broad categories, we find evidence that education affects fertility and economic opportunity related outcomes, but we find little evidence on other channels. 
It is crucial to highlight that while Zimbabwean independence brought about other changes beyond expanding secondary schooling (Thomas and Maluccio, 1996), we exploit the idea that being 13 years of age and younger in 1980 allowed one to have greater access to secondary school as opposed to being a few years older and therefore less likely to take advantage of the opportunity to attend secondary school; the operating assumption in this paper is that the other broader changes due to independence did not differentially affect those just below and those just above 13 in 1980. We assess the validity of this assumption by examining mortality outcomes of children born to mothers who had dropped out of school by age 9. These are mothers who did not take advantage of the educational reforms. Hence, if simply being of a certain age during independence is a driving factor for our results, we should find that children of those mothers below 13 in 1980 and those children born to slightly older mothers to have very different mortality outcomes even in this sample, however, we do not find any effect in this sample. In addition, we also include year of birth fixed-effects for the children born to these women, in order to account for any year-specific trends that could have affected all children. Finally, we also examine the trends in child mortality in Zimbabwe before and after independence and find that child mortality rates were relatively stable during the time period when the most of the births in our sample take place. 
Furthermore, we examine a few outcomes that are determined before independence, and outcomes that are not affected by education, and we find that just being of a certain age during independence is not driving our results. It should also be stressed that we examine the outcomes of children born many years after independence. Hence, if independence was accompanied by a better health system, then all children born after 1980 (regardless of when their mothers turned 13) would be exposed to the same postindependence health system. We find that even among children born many years after independence, those whose mothers happened to be below 13 in 1980 have better survival outcomes than those whose mothers were just too old to have benefited from the policy changes in the education sector. 
This paper is part of a number of studies that have looked specifically at the role of maternal education in improving health and fertility outcomes. Among developed countries, the opening of colleges to women in the United States has been linked to higher infant birth weight, increased gestational age at birth and lower fertility (Currie and Moretti, 2003; Grossman, 2006). Increases in compulsory years of education in Norway has also been linked to relatively large increases in weight at birth to children born to exposed women (Grytten et al., 2014; Lochner, 2011). However, children of women born in months just before primary school month-of-entry policies (who, hence, receive more education than women born in months just after such cut offs) in some parts of the United States were no healthier than babies born to women just after these cut offs, suggesting that not all gains in education have the same effect (Lochner, 2011; McCrary and Royer, 2011). 
In the developing world, a handful of studies have investigated the role of expanded access to primary schooling on child health and fertility outcomes. A policy that led to increased years of compulsory primary education in Turkey has been linked to lower fertility and increased use of contraception (Dinc¸ er et al., 2014) as well as less low birth weight babies and healthier children (Gunes, 2013). The introduction of universal primary education in Nigeria has been linked to lower early fertility among exposed women (Osili and Long, 2008). In a working paper, (Breierova and Duflo, 2004) use a large primary school construction program in Indonesia in the 1970s to find that female education was linked to delayed marriage and lower fertility, while the education of both parents was associated with lower child mortality. While our paper is similar in spirit, Breierova and Duflo (2004) have a rather weak first stage and are unable to examine detailed mechanisms. 
A few studies have also investigated the impact of higher levels of education on health outcomes in the developing world. In Taiwan, an increase in the number of years of compulsory schooling (from 6 to 9 years), affecting mostly junior high school aged children, coupled with a large school construction program has been linked to improved infant health outcomes, including a reduction in low birth weight children and a small decrease in infant mortality (Chou et al., 2010). A similar expansion of compulsory schooling from 6th to 9th grade in Mexico has been linked to increased use of contraception (Andalón et al., 2014). Evidence from the long-term follow-up of a large-scale randomized experiment which reduced the costs of education for adolescent girls in Kenya found that increased retention of students in schools was associated with lower teenage pregnancy rates (Duflo et al., 2014). Similarly, teenage girls in Malawi who were induced to stay in school through a cash transfer program also had lower teenage pregnancy rates (Baird et al., 2011). A study that investigated the impact of a large high school closure program in China, which uniquely employed an identification strategy from a policy change that led to lower levels of maternal education, found no impact of maternal education on child health outcomes (Zhang, 2012). It is notable that fertility rates in China at the time of this policy change were already very low by international standards. 
While all but two of the studies mentioned above support the view that increased education leads to improvements in child health, improved healthy behaviors, and lower fertility, none have looked specifically at the role of maternal secondary education that result from a national policy change in s setting with very high rates of child mortality and high rates of fertility (in Taiwan and China, rates of child mortality and fertility in the 1960s were already low by standards of most developing countries today). Rates of fertility and child mortality have remained persistently high in much of Sub-Saharan Africa, the same region where there have been the fewest gains in secondary schooling globally (Mingat et al., 2010). Given the importance of reducing child mortality globally, a better understanding of the role of maternal secondary education in improving child health and fertility in specifically these types of settings is thus important from a policy perspective. 
4.1. First stage results: Impact of policy changes on maternal education
Fig. 1 demonstrates the effects of the reforms on the educational attainment of all women in the DHS (not just those in our samples) with age of the women in 1980 presented along the $x$ -axis. There are two vertical lines in each figure at age 14 and 15, the ages that we consider partially treated. The women immediately on the left hand side of the vertical bars are considered the treatment group, and the women immediately on the right side are the control group (again we show data on all women in the DHS, not just those in our samples). The discontinuity is easily visible: women who were less than 13 in 1980 experienced a greater jump in schooling relative to those who were 16 or older in 1980. Table 2 summarizes the results of our first stage regressions: the discontinuity in education is associated with an increase in education of 1.68 years and an increased probability of attending some secondary school by 25.2 percentage points, after controlling for other variables. It is worth noting that these are very large and very rapid increases in both the number of year and probability of attending any secondary schooling. Table 2 also shows that the first stage is stronger in rural areas and among people with a low wealth index. In Appendix Fig. A1, we show that all provinces in Zimbabwe experienced increases in educational achievement and that this indeed was a country-wide expansion of schooling. We also include estimates of the impact of the educational policy on women who were 14 and 15 in 1980, and the effects are somewhat diminished, which makes sense since these women were only partially treated. In Table 2, we also demonstrate that our first stage results are also robust to the inclusion of polynomials in age in 1980 and with larger bandwidths in our sample. 
4.2. Impact on child survival
Fig. 2a charts the mortality of children born to all women in our sample. We see a discontinuous decrease in the probability of child death, which appears to coincide with the timing of the increases in maternal education. Note that the figures only tell us the difference in survival by the age of the mother in 1980, whereas the regressions, due to the instrumental variable approach, tell us the increase in survival due to an extra year of education, the results of which are presented in Table 3. One extra year of maternal education was associated with a 1.7 percentage point decrease in the probability that a child was reported to have died prior to the survey. Given that on average $7 . 9 \%$ of the children in our sample were reported to have died at the time of the survey, the decrease in child mortality from one year of education represents an approximately $2 1 . 5 \%$ decrease in child deaths. Table 3 also shows that OLS estimates of this relationship tend to be severely underestimated. Additionally, Table 3 reveals that the effects on mortality by a certain age (5 years or 1 year) are largely similar, negative and statistically significant. Child deaths are heavily concentrated in the early years of life, which is consistent with known patterns of child mortality. This is important, since our primary measure of child survival includes all children ever born to the women in the DHS surveys, including children who may be teenagers or adults by the later surveys. We also test an alternative outcome – mortality by age 5 and mortality by age 1 – to rule out, for example, the rise of HIV among youth.

In Fig. 2b and Table 4 we present the disaggregated results for women according to geographic area and by low vs. high wealth index. Our findings suggest that the majority of gains in both education and child survival were concentrated among women living in rural areas and among the poorest women, our second stage estimates are only significant among rural and low-wealth index women (although sample sizes are much smaller when disaggregated), which is consistent with what is known about the rollout of the educational reforms4. This pattern appears to be true for all measures of mortality. 
We conduct a number of robustness checks on our mortality results. These results are presented in Tables 5a and 5b. First, since schooling may have delayed the age of first birth for some women, we also run our tests excluding data from the 1988 survey, a time when some of the women in our sample may still be in school. Second, since our preferred specification excludes women who were exactly 14 and 15 in 1980, we also test whether including these women alters our results. Third, we test more narrow age bands (11–18 and 12–17), further tightening our sample to women who were just too old or too young to benefit from the policy. Finally, as is common in the RD literature, we test whether non-linear age trends in the data affect our results (see Table 5b, middle panel). In addition, as Table 5b shows, the results are robust to including birth year times survey year fixed effects and using age in 1980 interacted with region of residence as an instrument. Our findings are robust to all of these alternative samples and specifications. 
Appendix Table A1 additionally shows that by restricting the sample to: (1) women who are more likely to have completed their fertility by the date of the survey (aged above 35), (2) by child sex, (3) by early birth order (these results are also seen in Fig. 2c) and (4) to births occurring 5 years prior to the survey to minimize issues with recall, the results are barely altered. Again, our findings are robust to all of these alternative samples and specifications. 
4.3. Channels linking education to survival outcomes
Next, we explore the mechanisms through which increased education might be associated with changes in child mortality, which are grouped into broad categories and presented in Tables 6–8. We also present reduced form estimates for all of the same outcomes in Appendix Tables A2a–A2c. As an additional robustness check, we also present the results for the same outcomes in Tables 6 and 7 in Appendix Table A3 with a wider bandwidth in age (to allow for polynomials) and with polynomials of age in 1980. The first set of mechanisms that we explore is the impact of expanded access to maternal education on the fertility and sexual behavior of women, the results of which are presented in Table 6 (reduced form OLS estimates for all mechanisms we explore are also presented in Appendix Tables A2a–A2c). We find that increased secondary schooling delays age at first cohabitation, perhaps consequently age at first intercourse and also the age at first birth; an additional year of schooling delays entry into cohabitation by 0.5 years, off a base of 19 years. The delay in terms of having the first child is smaller at around 0.3 years, but it is perhaps mediated by the fact that more schooling reduces the time between marriage and first child. The middle panel of Column 2 in Table 6 shows that schooling also has an impact on the number of children born (including children that eventually die); however, this result is not statistically significant for women close to the end of their fertile cycle (35 years), though the magnitude is similar. We interpret this result with some caution since this is not robust to polynomials in age, the results of which are shown in Appendix Table A3. As seen in Fig. 3, there appears to be little evidence of a strong trend break in number of children in the reduced form.

Studies have shown that first births and births to younger mothers are of much higher mortality rates than other births (Hobcraft, 1993). Using data from 25 DHS data from the 1990s, Hobcraft estimated that the excess risk for firstborn children to teenage mothers was on the order of $4 0 \%$ (Hobcraft et al., 1985). Data from Texas, which has much lower rates of child mortality than in our sample, suggests that children who were born to women less than 18 years old had a $3 0 \%$ higher risk of being born preterm as compared to children born to mothers just a few years older (Royer, 2004). Increased age at first birth, even of a small amount, during this critical time period, could potentially be an important channel that links increased maternal education to reduced child mortality. To further investigate the impact of increased education on the age distribution of women enter cohabitation and motherhood, we investigate whether the policy was associated with the probability of giving birth by age of 16 (an age where students might be still be expected to be in high school) and age 20 (an age where most students would not be expected to be in high school). As presented in Table 6 (bottom panel), we see that the policy was associated with a lower probability of giving birth by age 16 but not age 20, which suggests that the increase in age at birth was concentrated among girls in their late teenage years.

Desired fertility, on the other hand, does seem to be strongly affected by schooling. Women with more schooling desire fewer children and, as Table 6 suggests, 0.22 children less off a base of 4.5 children. This result is robust to polynomial trends on either side of the discontinuity, and importantly, as can be seen from Fig. 3, there appears to be a real trend break around the discontinuity in the number of children desired. 
We then examine outcomes such as female empowerment (see Section 2 and Appendix A on how this variable is created), opinions on domestic violence and risky behavior (smoking) in Table 7, and we find very little evidence that schooling plays an important role in affecting these outcomes. In the lower half of Table 7, we examine economic outcomes of the mother and find that asset ownership, urban location, and probability of working are higher for the more educated. We also define two dummy variables: one for being in the lowest wealth quintile and another for being in the highest wealth quintile. We find that more education is associated with a lower probability of being in the lowest wealth quintile, which is likely related to the changes in the probability of having electricity and living in a rural area. Appendix Table A3 shows that the coefficients on urban location and probability of working are robust to polynomial trends in age; however, we wish to stress some caution in reading too much into these results as the reduced form results in Fig. 4 do not show clear trend breaks in these outcomes around the cutoff. Taken together, we interpret the results of Tables 6 and 7 to suggest that schooling appears to affect fertility decisions and some economic outcomes that might be relevant for child survival. Education does not seem to affect outcomes related to the woman’s status in the household, although we admit that these are relatively noisy measures in the DHS. 
It is important to also examine the impact of the reform on male schooling in order to determine if the results we observe are being driven by maternal education, paternal education or changes in marriage match quality that result after the school expansion. While it is rather difficult to completely disentangle these effects, we explore these possibilities in Fig. 5 and conclude that changes in maternal education are most likely driving our results. A few things are important to note in Fig. 5. First, the age specificity of the reform is less strict for men, both due to pre-independence higher rates of school attendance and perhaps also a higher likelihood of re-enrolling after independence. Second, there is no discontinuity in the age of men that women marry, and while the reform also affected men, the fact that age of husbands does not shift around the cutoff (note that women marry men who are on average 5 years older) suggests that husband’s education is likely not that different around the cutoff (bottom left in Fig. 5). Third, the bottom-right graph in Fig. 5 shows that, consistent with this idea, the most significant change among couples wherein the woman is below 14 or 15 in 1980 is the difference in educational attainment between husbands and wives. Women who experienced increased schooling have a smaller gap in educational attainment compared to women who were too old to take advantage of the reforms. These figures lend support to the notion that changes in maternal education around the time of the reform are the most important drivers of the educational reforms on child mortality. We also recognize that these results can be interpreted as suggesting that what might matter for outcomes is not maternal education per se but the difference in husband versus wife’s education. We cannot disentangle these effects in the current design.

In Table 8, maternal education appears to have no statistically significant effect on institutional deliveries (which is surprising given the apparent jump visible in Fig. 6), but it has a large and statistically significant effect on delivery via cesarean section. The cesarean section rate in our sample is relatively low, but the magnitude of the result suggests that an extra year of education increases the likelihood of a cesarean section by nearly $8 5 \%$ . Interestingly, maternal education seems to have no effect on preventive health care acts such as antenatal care and full immunizations, although these rates are high in the full sample. These results are shown graphically in Fig. 6. 
Using household surveys to measure health service utilization has been shown to be prone to different forms of bias, however, with no clear generalizable patterns for the bias, in particular in low and middle income countries (Miles et al., 2013). While we might expect women of higher education to more accurately report health service use, the fact that we observe little significant effects of education on any of the health service utilization variables we investigate causes us to believe recall bias didn’t have a major effect in driving our findings on health service utilization. Where reporting biases has been analyzed in the context of diarrhea, investigators found very little differences in accuracy of reported rates between mothers with primary and secondary education, the most relevant margin for this study (Boerma et al., 1991). 
It is worth highlighting that we test health-seeking behaviors among the most recent births but child mortality in all births of the women in our sample. We therefore replicate our child mortality results in the sample of recent births in the bottom panel of Table 8. We find that the results are very similar in magnitude but and statistically significant at conventional levels despite diminished sample size (Table 8 uses only $2 0 \%$ of the data used in Table 3). 
4.4. Robustness tests
As stated earlier, an important concern is that independence led to other changes, beyond those in the education sector—although why this would occur differentially for those just below and above 13 is perhaps a harder argument to make. Our first attempt at capturing the possibility that other reforms or changes might differentially affect those below 13 in 1980 is to examine our main child mortality outcome for women who had dropped out by age 9 and, hence, never took advantage of the reforms (we repeated this exercise for dropouts by age 10 or 11 and the results looked very similar)5. Fig. 7 shows that there is no discontinuity in child mortality around age 14 or 15 for women who dropped out of school years before independence. Hence, it seems unlikely that independence led to other reforms that had age-specific effects that could explain our results. 
We also plot child mortality relative to the year of birth of the children in Fig. 8. We see that there were large declines in child mortality that began in the mid-1970s but that by 1983, child mortality rates had largely stabilized and remained relatively constant through the 2000s. There is no discontinuity in child mortality at independence itself, rather the continuation in a trend that began prior to independence. This pattern is consistent with a decline in mortality as the country transitioned out of civil war before independence and which researchers predicted in the 1980s, despite the lack of data to measure child mortality at the time. It is also consistent with evidence from the 1980s that suggested that most of the changes within the health care sector happened within the first few years after independence, but then stabilized or eventually faded out (Loewenson et al., 1991). Since the average age of first birth of the women in our sample was 19 years old, most of the births in our sample of children would have occurred after the major declines in child mortality in this figure occurred. 
Given that the women in our control group were slightly older than women in the treatment group it may be that more children of control group mothers were born during a time period with higher prevailing child mortality rates, which could account for some of the differences in child mortality we observe between groups. To help rule this out, we conduct two additional analyses, which are presented in Appendix Tables A4a and A4b. First, we update our main results on child mortality on samples that exclude all children born in or prior to the years 1980 through 1985. Given that the average year of birth in our sample is in the late 1980s, unsurprisingly we don’t lose a lot of sample by excluding these additional children and our estimates remain largely unchanged, suggesting our main findings are not being driven by births that occurred during the early 1980s. Second, we also do a similar exercise but instead exclude all women whose first births occurred in or prior to the years 1980 through 1985. This is a much stricter restriction on our sample and so we face a much greater challenge in maintaining sample size, but again we find that the results are relatively consistent to our main findings and robust to this additional test. 
Given that our findings suggest that the reduction in child mortality was driven primarily through a delay in the age of first birth, we further investigate the changes in fertility in our samples. At independence, contraceptive rates were relatively low in Zimbabwe, and the national family planning program had been deemed largely inefficient. Although family planning programs had existed in Zimbabwe since 1964, only about $1 4 \%$ of married women aged 15–49 were using any form of modern contraception at independence (Boohene and Dow, 1987). In 1981, the family planning programs were reorganized and integrated within the Ministry of Health and over the next few years underwent significant operational changes. They also adopted a new strategy that focused heavily on community-based distribution, information and education, as well as improvements in the supply of modern family planning programs (Boohene and Dow, 1987). By the mid-1980s, the contraceptive prevalence rate among married women had increased substantially; however, there was very little observed decline in fertility overall, suggesting that most of the increased use of contraceptives was likely being used by women to increase birth spacing (Boohene and Dow, 1987). In Table 7, we found a small effect of increased education on the use of modern contraception that was only significant at the $1 0 \%$ level. Furthermore, in Fig. 9, we chart the trends in birth spacing among the women in our sample. While there is a clear trend toward longer birth intervals among younger women, there is no discontinuity visible at the ages corresponding to the increase in education. Our findings strongly suggest that the biggest effect of increased maternal education on fertility appears to have been largely mechanical: an important shift in the age at cohabitation and of first birth. And, while education appears to have affected the desire to have fewer children, we find little effect on actual fertility, suggesting that access to contraception was unlikely to have been made available in an age-specific manner that could be undermining our results. 
While it is common for regression discontinuity design based papers to show “smoothness of covariates”, this is particularly difficult to do in our case since the DHS does not collect data on a rich set of variables that were likely to be determined prior to early adolescence. Hence, almost any outcome in the DHS could have been affected by increased education. 
In Fig. 10, we show that sex of the child, the religion of the mother, the number of women sampled and the year of survey are quite smooth around the point of discontinuity. Moreover, show that height of the mother (determined in early childhood) is also not affected around the discontinuity. 
A related concern is that of differential mortality of the mother: if the more educated are less (or more) likely to be surveyed or less (or more) likely to die, then such sample selection could bias our results. Even though Appendix Fig. A2 shows that the histogram of ages is smooth around the cutoff, we further explore this possibility in a few different ways. First, using the module on maternal mortality in the DHS, we examine the likelihood of the female siblings of the respondent dying due to pregnancy-related reasons by age of the sister in 1980 and conditional on the sister being alive in 1980. Appendix Fig. A3 suggests that differential pregnancy-related mortality due to increased education is likely not an issue. Using data on all siblings (male or female) of the respondent in Appendix Fig. A4, we assess the probability that any individual sibling is alive in 1980 and whether any individual sibling is likely to be dead after 1980 conditional on being alive in 1980 (in other words, the top right graph of Appendix Fig. A4 shows that there does not appear to be a discontinuity in adult mortality as a result of increased education). In the bottom panel of Appendix Fig. A4, we examine whether sibling structure is differential around the cutoff and find that it is not. These figures help add evidence suggesting that what was mainly different for those just below and just above the age of 13 in 1980 was access to schooling. 
Finally, evidence presented in using data from neighboring countries shows that not all countries experienced an increase in educational enrollment starting in the 1980s (see Fig. 5. 
5. Discussion and conclusions
A number of channels have been postulated as ways in which education might lead to changes in child mortality and other health outcomes. First, education may make people more efficient producers of health (the productive efficiency hypothesis), which could make people more adherent to treatments or make people get more health out of the same inputs. Second, education may make people more aware of different treatments, which may lead them to make different choices with regards to preventive behaviors or different treatments (the allocative efficiency hypothesis). Third, increased education might lead to higher incomes, which could directly affect health by increasing the purchasing power of individuals (income effects). Finally, there are also a number of additional pathways, which might influence one or more of these channels in less wellestablished ways, such as the propensity to marry a better partner, social networks, etc. 
Our study provides causal evidence of the impact of maternal education on child mortality and explores some potential channels underlying this effect. We find a robust effect on child survival and find supporting evidence toward channels that involve fertility and economic outcomes due to increased education. We do not find support for mechanisms that involve health-seeking behavior or female empowerment. It is notable that these effects seem to have occurred in a setting where the quality of secondary education is reportedly quite poor. Our findings suggest that education may have largely had an incapacitation effect, in the sense that it mechanically delayed age at cohabitation and fertility. Had the quality of education been improved, there might have been additional effects, and it might also help to explain little changes in health-seeking behavior or other outcomes. 
We note that the educational reforms in Zimbabwe differed from many of the previous natural experiments investigated that were compulsory in nature, since enrolling into secondary school was still voluntary post-Independence. Compulsory schooling and voluntary expansions to schooling will capture different local average treatment effect (LATE). In a world of compulsory schooling, it is possible that those children whose returns to schooling are the lowest are now the marginal children who are pushed (by law) into attending school. In our case, the children on the margin might be children whose returns to schooling might be high, but costs also sufficiently high, that prior to the reforms, these children did not go to school. Hence, when costs are lowered and schooling is not compulsory, those with the highest returns might select into schooling, while those with low returns, despite the lower costs, choose to stay out of school. Hence, compulsory schooling and expanded access to schooling measures capture two rather different LATEs depending on the assumptions we make about how each of the reforms change the returns to and costs of schooling. 
Those familiar with Zimbabwe are undoubtedly aware of other changes that have happened in this country over the past few decades, including the rapid spread of HIV and the near collapse of the public health system (Todd et al., 2010). However, we believe our empirical approach can rule out most of these changes as potential alternative explanations due to the age-specific structure of our analysis. While another study suggested that Zimbabwean women who benefited from the same policy change were more likely to be aware of HIV and its modes of transmission, there is no evidence that these women were more or less likely to acquire HIV/AIDS. 
At the time of independence, Zimbabwe was a well-off country relative to other countries in Sub-Saharan Africa; it had relatively high levels of literacy and high levels of primary education, but secondary education rates were still low. Moreover, under-5 mortality in Zimbabwe in 1980 was approximately 100 per 1000 live births, and the total fertility rate was just over 7, which would make it comparable to the rates in many African countries today. Like improving health, increasing education is also an important development priority. Millennium Development Goal (MDG) 2, a goal to achieve universal primary education, has been among the most successful MDGs: primary education enrollment in developing regions reached nearly $9 0 \%$ by 2011 (Go and Quijada, 2012). Primary education rates for girls have improved the most and are now nearly on par with those of boys, even in the poorest regions in the world. However, there has been substantially less progress with regards to secondary education, in particular in the least developed countries of Sub-Saharan Africa, where child mortality rates remain high (Mingat et al., 2010). Our findings suggest that increasing maternal secondary education might be an effective way to reduce child mortality and fertility rates in the developing world today.
