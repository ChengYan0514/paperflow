This paper reviews the economic research on obesity, covering topics such as the measurement of, and trends in, obesity, the economic causes of obesity (e.g. the monetary price and time cost of food, food assistance programs, income, education, macroeconomic conditions, and peer effects), and the economic consequences of obesity (e.g. lower wages, a lower probability of employment, and higher medical care costs). It also examines the extent to which obesity imposes negative externalities, and economic interventions that could potentially internalize such externalities, such as food taxes, subsidies for school-based physical activity programs, and financial rewards for weight loss. It discusses other economic rationales for government intervention with respect to obesity, such as imperfect information, time inconsistent preferences, and irrational behavior. It concludes by proposing a research agenda for the field. 
Overall, the evidence suggests that there is no single dominant economic cause of obesity; a wide variety of factors may contribute a modest amount to the risk. There is consistent evidence regarding the economic consequences of obesity, which are lower wages and higher medical care costs that impose negative externalities through health insurance. Studies of economic approaches to preventing obesity, such as menu labeling, taxes on energy-dense foods, and financial rewards for weight loss find only modest effects on weight and thus a range of policies may be necessary to have a substantial effect on the prevalence of obesity.

1. Introduction
The prevalence of obesity has risen dramatically in the United States and most other economically developed countries in the past several decades (National Center for Health Statistics, 2014; OECD, 2014). Concern about the health risks, medical care costs, and other consequences of obesity has led to a host of policies and programs implemented by companies, school districts, and governments to prevent and reduce obesity. It has also resulted in a large body of economic research on the causes and consequences of obesity and on approaches to prevent and reduce obesity. The purpose of this article is to organize and synthesize what is known from this literature and to identify the most pressing needs and promising opportunities for future research. 
The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the World Health Organization define obesity in adults as a body mass index or BMI (which is calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared) of 30 or higher, and define obesity in youth as a weight-for-height that exceeds the historic 95th percentile (Institute of Medicine, 2012; U.S. DHHS, 2010; WHO, 2000) (see Table 1 for the definitions of each clinical weight classification). BMI does not measure fatness, merely weight for height, so individuals with above-average lean mass (e.g. muscular individuals) may be incorrectly classified as obese (Burkhauser and Cawley, 2008). More accurate measures of obesity are based on fat mass rather than total body mass, and these measures are in some cases more strongly correlated with outcomes than BMI.1 Although a few data sets, such as the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) contain measurements of body composition (such as fat mass and fat free mass), most social science datasets continue to include only weight and height. As a result, most of the research discussed in this review defines obesity using BMI.

Trends in the prevalence of obesity in the U.S. have been tracked for more than 50 years using data on BMI calculated using measured weight and height in the series of NHANES surveys. Americans began gaining weight in the 1920s but this did not result in large increases in obesity until decades later: the 1960s or 1970s if one defines obesity using percent body fat or the 1980s if one defines obesity using BMI (Komlos and Brabec, 2010; Burkhauser et al., 2009). Specifically, the prevalence of obesity (defined using BMI) among U.S. adults rose slowly from $1 3 . 3 \%$ in 1960–1962 to $1 5 . 1 \%$ in 1976–1980. From that point on, it rose dramatically, from $1 5 . 1 \%$ in 1976–1980 to $2 3 . 3 \%$ in 1988–1994 to $3 5 . 3 \%$ in 2007–2010 (National Center for Health Statistics, 2014); see Fig. 1. 2 The prevalence of obesity in youths follows a similar pattern; see Fig. 2. The increase in obesity is particularly striking because it happened at the same time as many healthier trends such as decreased smoking and reduced consumption of alcohol, as well as decreased drug use

Obesity also rose in many other countries of the world during the same period, but the prevalence in the U.S. remains one of the highest. Among the large economically developed countries that are members of The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), the U.S. has the highest prevalence of adult obesity $( 3 5 . 3 \% )$ , followed by Mexico $( 3 2 . 4 \% )$ , New Zealand $( 3 1 . 3 \% )$ , Hungary $( 2 8 . 5 \% )$ , Australia $( 2 8 . 3 \% )$ , Canada $( 2 5 . 4 \% )$ , Chile $( 2 5 . 1 \% )$ , the United Kingdom $( 2 4 . 7 \% )$ and Ireland $( 2 3 . 0 \% )$ ; see OECD (2014). 3 East Asian countries such as Japan and Korea have the lowest prevalence $3 . 6 \%$ and $4 . 6 \%$ , respectively); see OECD (2014). The dozen or so countries with higher rates of obesity than the U.S. include small Pacific island nations like Nauru, Tonga, and Samoa, where the prevalence exceeds $5 0 \%$ (World Health Organization, 2011). The Institute of Medicine and the World Health Organization describe current rates of obesity as epidemic (Institute of Medicine, 2012; World Health Organization, 2011). 
The daily calorie surplus that explains the increase in weights in the U.S. is relatively small: 220 calories per day for adults (Hall et al., 2011) and 41 calories per day for youth (Wang et al., 2012).4 Unfortunately, there are very little direct data on calories consumed and calories burned over time, and what does exist is measured with substantial error. For example, one source of data on calories consumed is from 24-h dietary recall; these were collected as part of the Continuing Survey of Food Intakes by Individuals (CSFII) and continue to be collected as part of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys (NHANES). However, these reports are plagued by significant reporting error. Archer et al. (2013) estimate that food intake in the NHANES is underreported by 281 calories per day for men and 365 calories per day for women (i.e. greater than the calorie surplus that explains the rise in weight over time in the U.S.), and describe the food intake data for a majority of NHANES respondents as “not physiologically plausible” (p. 8), i.e. incompatible with survival. Changes in measurement protocols and potential changes in reporting error over time further complicate efforts to measure trends in energy intake (Archer et al., 2013). Data on calories expended are limited to occasional time use surveys that record only activities, not actual energy expenditure. 
Data on weight and obesity have their own challenges and limitations. Most datasets used by economists contain only self-reports (not measurements) of weight and height. The NHANES data, which contain both self-reports and measurements of weight and height, indicate that the reporting error in weight is non-classical; specifically, individuals tend to underreport their weight, with heavier individuals underreporting to a greater extent (see, e.g. Cawley et al., 2015).5 Such non-classical reporting error can bias regression coefficients in both models in which weight is the dependent variable and models in which weight is a regressor, and it can be difficult to sign the bias (Bound et al., 2002). 
One way to address reporting error in weight is to use the NHANES as validation data (Cawley, 2004; Cawley and Burkhauser, 2006; Bound et al., 2002). Specifically, one can regress measured weight on self-reported weight in the NHANES and then “transport” the NHANES coefficients to the dataset of interest and use them to correct self-reported weight for reporting error.6 This assumes “transportability” (that the nature of the reporting error is the same in both datasets) and does not completely eliminate reporting error (O’Neill and Sweetman, 2013; Cawley and Burkhauser, 2006). The rise in obesity has serious implications for public health. Excess fat is harmful because fat cells are collectively an endocrine organ, releasing hormones such as resistin, which causes insulin resistance and diabetes, and leptin, which damages the cardiovascular system (Trayhurn and Beattie, 2001; Hu, 2008).7 The pancreas compensates for insulin resistance by releasing even more insulin, which in turn increases the risk of various forms of cancer (Calle and Kaaks, 2004). It is estimated that obesity is responsible for $1 7 . 3 \%$ of coronary heart disease, $6 1 \%$ of Type II diabetes, $2 4 \%$ of osteoarthritis, $2 0 . 8 \substack { - 3 5 . 4 \% }$ of colorectal cancers, $2 6 . 9 \%$ of pancreatic cancer, $3 5 . 5 \%$ of gallbladder cancer, and $4 2 . 5 \%$ of kidney cancer (Eckel, 2003; Calle and Kaaks, 2004). The greatest health impact results from morbid obesity $\left( \mathsf { B M I } \geq 3 5 \right)$ ; those just over the threshold of obesity $3 0 \leq \mathsf { B M I } < 3 5$ , which is called class 1 obesity, do not tend to fare worse than those of healthy weight in terms of medical care costs (Cawley and Meyerhoefer, 2012) or mortality risk (Mehta and Chang, 2009; Flegal et al., 2013). The health impact of morbid obesity is substantial, however, resulting in a roughly onethird higher risk of mortality (Flegal et al., 2013).8 In light of these health consequences, a White House Task Force described obesity as a “national health crisis” for the U.S. (White House Task Force on Childhood Obesity, 2010). 
Economists took note of the rise in obesity and its implications, and research on the subject increased dramatically. According to the EconLit database of journal articles, dissertations, and working papers, the number of annual economics publications with the keyword “obesity” rose from zero in the year 1995 to four in 2000, 48 in 2005, and 152 in 2010. The economics of obesity, on which there was virtually no research 20 years ago, has become such an active topic that it is challenging to stay abreast of the literature. Given the size of the literature, this review is by necessity selective; for example, it focuses on the studies with the most credible and interesting identification strategies and excludes from consideration unpublished working papers because their results may change. 
The paper is structured as follows. Section 2 discusses evidence on the economic causes of obesity, such as the money price and time cost of food, food assistance programs, income, education, the macroeconomy, and peer effects. Section 3 discusses the economic consequences of obesity, such as wages, employment, and medical care costs. Section 4 discusses possible mechanisms by which obesity may impose negative externalities and decrease social welfare. Section 5 examines the research on possible mechanisms for internalizing such externalities, and Section 6 discusses the evidence for other economic rationales for government intervention, such as imperfect information and consumers having time inconsistent preferences or behaving irrationally. Section 7 offers an economic research agenda on obesity, noting the greatest needs and most promising opportunities for future research. 
2.1. The money price and time cost of food
In the past several decades, the real prices of energy-dense foods (i.e. foods with a high number of calories per unit weight) have fallen relative to the prices of less energy-dense, nutritious foods. For example, between 1990 and 2007, the real price of a 2-L bottle of Coca Cola fell $3 4 . 9 \%$ and the real price of a 12-in. pizza at Pizza Hut fell $1 7 . 2 \%$ (Christian and Rashad, 2009) while the prices of fresh fruits and vegetables rose faster than inflation (Wendt and Todd, 2011; Sturm, 2005b). If these types of food are normal goods, then consumers likely responded to these price changes by increasing their consumption of energy-dense foods and decreasing their consumption of fresh fruits and vegetables. A large number of papers have regressed obesity or BMI on local food prices; the limitation of this approach is that variation in local food prices may be due to differences in demand over geography and changes in demand over time and thus the estimates of the impact of local food prices on weight may be biased. 
A few papers have exploited natural experiments to estimate the effect of food prices on weight. The impact of fast food prices on weight was examined by Cotti and Tefft (2013), which uses the state minimum wage as an instrument for county fast food prices. The logic is that the fast food industry predominantly employs minimum-wage workers, so an increase in minimum wages should to some extent be passed on to consumers in the form of higher food prices. Their IV models (which control for income and employment to address any income effect of minimum wage laws on weight) yield no evidence that fast food prices contribute to obesity; the estimated effects are small, in many cases of the opposite sign than predicted, and statistically insignificant. They also cannot reject the null hypothesis of no effect of their fast food price index on the self-reported consumption of fast food. 
Others have focused on the price of groceries as opposed to fast food. Courtemanche and Carden (2011) indirectly examine the effect of grocery prices on weight by examining the impact of Wal-Mart Supercenters on the weight of local residents. Wal-Mart has become the largest supermarket chain in the U.S. through aggressive price competition. Generally, the opening of a store would not be useful for identification because it would be a result of demand, but Walmart pursued a strategy of expanding slowly outward in concentric circles from its corporate headquarters in Arkansas. The IV models in Courtemanche and Carden (2011), which use an interaction of distance from corporate headquarters and time as an instrument for the opening of a Wal-Mart store, suggest that an additional Wal-Mart Supercenter per 100,000 residents increases local BMI by 0.24 units, and increases the risk of obesity by $2 . 3 \%$ points, which seems large. These are reduced-form IV models; weight is regressed on the presence of a Wal-Mart Superstore without observing the intermediate steps of grocery purchases or food consumption. 
Others have studied the influence of the time cost of acquiring fast food, measured by proximity to fast food outlets. Two studies use features of the interstate highway system as instruments for fast food density or distance in order to estimate the causal effects of fast food availability on weight. Dunn (2010) uses the number of interstate highway exits as an instrument for number of fast food restaurants in a county, with the rationale that nearby highway exit ramps imply more visitor traffic that supports additional fast food outlets for reasons that have nothing to do with the demand of residents. Dunn (2010) cannot reject the null hypothesis of no impact of availability or density of fast food outlets on the BMI of white residents in low-density or high-density counties, but finds some evidence that the number of outlets increases BMI among women and non-whites in medium-density counties. Anderson and Matsa (2011) use a somewhat similar approach, using distance to an interstate highway as an instrument for the distance to fast food restaurants, at a fine level of geographic detail (area code and exchange, and zip code) in rural counties of 11 states. The twosample IV models yield no evidence that restaurant access affects BMI, obesity, or overweight; the authors examine food diary data and conclude that the increased calorie consumption from restaurant meals is offset with lower calorie consumption over the rest of the day. 
Currie et al. (2010) examine the association of schoolchild obesity and the school’s proximity to fast food restaurants. Two strengths of the study are the large sample size (over 3 million ninth-graders in California) and data on measured body fat. The authors compare students whose schools are within 0.1 mile, to those whose schools are within 0.25 mile, of a fast food restaurant and find that students whose school is closer to a fast food restaurant have a prevalence of obesity (defined using body fat) that is $1 . 7 \%$ points $( 5 . 2 \% )$ higher. They estimate that this is consistent with an additional 30–100 calories consumed per school day, which seems large as an intention to treat estimate – the number of additional calories consumed by the children who actually eat at the fast food restaurant that day must be much larger, especially when one allows for some degree of offsetting behavior (eating less at other times of day as a result). There is no difference in the prevalence of obesity between students who attend schools that are 0.25 miles compared to 0.5 miles from a fast food restaurant; it is unclear whether this should be reason for skepticism about the earlier result, or should be interpreted as an indication of the degree of proximity that matters. The authors acknowledge that they have no exogenous variation in distance to fast food restaurants, and thus the estimates could be biased by unmeasured differences in the demand for fast food. They argue that this bias is relatively small for several reasons: they are comparing small distances, a falsification test indicates that proximity to new future fast food restaurants is not associated with obesity, and proximity to nonfast-food restaurants is uncorrelated with weight. 
The results of these studies are inconsistent; overall, there is weak evidence that cheaper food (in terms of money price or time cost) contributes to obesity. However, this literature is limited by a scarcity of powerful and valid instruments for prices and time costs and generally missing data on the intermediate step of food consumption. 
2.2. Food assistance programs
The U.S. has many government programs that subsidize food, such as the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), the National School Lunch Program (NSLP), and the School Breakfast Program (SBP). Although they were originally designed to reduce hunger and under-nutrition, they may have the unintended consequence of increasing obesity for certain individuals. For example, benefits in the SNAP program can be spent on the full range of groceries; only prepared food (such as hot rotisserie chicken or sandwiches) and alcohol are excluded, which means that the program subsidizes energy-dense (as well as other) groceries. For example, grocery store scanner data suggest that SNAP pays for roughly $\$ 2$ billion worth of sugar-sweetened beverages annually (Andreyeva et al., 2012). 
The National School Lunch Program (NSLP) may also have unintended consequences. Part of the motivation for the NSLP was that many U.S. draftees during World War II were rejected for being stunted or malnourished; subsidized school lunches were seen as a way to increase the weight and height of American youth and thereby increase national security by ensuring a larger supply of healthy potential soldiers during the Cold War. Ironically, the NSLP has recently been criticized by the military for increasing youth obesity and thereby decreasing military readiness (Mission: Readiness, 2010). 
A large number of studies have examined the association of participation in these programs with obesity. However, it has proven challenging to identify the causal effect of the NSLP and SNAP on obesity because there has not been random assignment to the programs and it is difficult to observe a valid control group (Meyerhoefer and Yang, 2011). Studies employing creative identification strategies provide important information on the effects of these programs. For example, Schanzenbach (2009) estimates a regression discontinuity model that compares children whose family income is just below $1 8 5 \%$ of the Federal Poverty Line (and who thus are eligible for subsidized school lunches) to those whose family income is just above that threshold (who must pay full price for a school lunch). Clearly, the running variable (income) may directly affect weight but income is assumed to affect weight in a smooth manner, and not discontinuously at the eligibility cutoff for school lunch. Dividing the estimated impact of eligibility on weight outcomes by the estimated increase in school lunch take-up around the threshold, Schanzenbach estimates that school lunch increases the likelihood of obesity by $4 3 \%$ by the end of first grade. (A Wald estimator calculated using the mean difference in lunch eating rates on either side of the subsidy cutoff implies that school lunch increases the risk of obesity by less – $2 0 \%$ .) These large estimates may be due to the youths’ families becoming eligible for other programs (such as school breakfast) at the same income cutoff. In contrast to these results for first grade, there is no significant impact of the NSLP on obesity at the end of fifth grade. 
Bhattacharya et al. (2006) estimate the impact of the School Breakfast Program (SBP) using a difference-in-differences model that compares the difference in nutritional outcomes between the school year and summer for students at schools that do and do not offer the SBP. The estimates suggest that the SBP does not increase total calories (it actually decreases calories from fat) but does improve the nutritional quality of children’s diets. Given that their identification strategy relies on relatively short-term seasonal variation, they do not expect to detect an impact on BMI and in fact do not. 
Millimet et al. (2010) examine the relationship between participation in both the NSLP and the SBP during the spring of 1st grade on weight in the spring of 3rd grade using data from the Early Childhood Longitudinal Study – Kindergarten Class of 1998–1999. Estimates from bivariate probit models with different assumptions about selection into the program suggest that even a modest amount of selection implies that the SBP lowers weight and the NSLP raises child weight, which is generally consistent with both Schanzenbach (2009) and Bhattacharya et al. (2006). 
Meyerhoefer and Pylypchuk (2008) investigate the relationship between SNAP receipt and obesity using the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey. In order to account for non-random selection into SNAP they incorporate instruments that measure state-level outreach program expenditures for SNAP into a discrete factor model that controls for unobserved heterogeneity. They find no relationship between SNAP receipt and obesity among men, but their estimates suggest that SNAP participation raises the probability of overweight and obesity by $5 . 9 \%$ among women. 
Kaushal (2007) estimates the impact of SNAP receipt on one specific population – immigrants – by exploiting the fact that after the 1996 Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act (PRWORA), foreign born unmarried mothers’ eligibility for food stamps varied across states and over time. Kaushal estimates a two-sample model of instrumental variables that exploits this policy variation; the estimates indicate that food stamp participation raises adult BMI by a statistically insignificant $0 . 3 \%$ . 
The literature on food assistance programs and obesity defies easy summary. Results differ by method, sex, and grade. One consistent finding, however, is that the SBP is more protective against obesity than the NSLP, which is consistent with other studies that have found that school breakfasts tend to comply with statutory limits on saturated fat while school lunches tend to exceed them, and that skipping breakfast is associated with higher overall calorie consumption (e.g. Millimet et al., 2010; Morgan et al., 1986). 
The aforementioned studies seek to identify a point estimate of the causal effect. A different approach is taken by Gundersen et al. (2012) and Kreider et al. (2012), who estimate bounds on the treatment effect of NSLP and SNAP, respectively, using partial identification methods that address the problems of endogenous selection into participation and the underreporting of participation. Their results suggest that the NSLP and SNAP are protective against youth obesity. Utilizing three control groups (children whose household income exceeds the eligibility threshold; incomeeligible children enrolled in schools that do not participate in NSLP, which are largely private schools; and income-eligible children who have dropped out of school), and employing different assumptions about selection and measurement error, Gundersen et al. (2012) estimate that the NSLP reduces obesity by at least $3 . 2 \%$ points or $1 7 \%$ . Kreider et al. (2012) estimate that SNAP reduces the prevalence of youth obesity by at least $5 . 3 \%$ points. 
2.3. Income
In general, there is a robust negative correlation between weight and income among women but not men; i.e. higher-income women are less likely to be obese (Sobal and Stunkard, 1989; McLaren, 2007). For example, in the NHANES data for 2005–2008, the prevalence of obesity among adult women was $2 9 . 0 \%$ for women living in a household with a poverty-to-income ratio (PIR) greater than $3 5 0 \%$ but $4 2 . 0 \%$ for those living in a household with a PIR of less than $1 3 0 \%$ (Ogden et al., 2010). 
Research in economics has sought to determine the causal impact of income on weight. In theory, additional income could either increase or decrease weight. Income could lead to weight gain if food and sedentary pursuits are normal goods. However, good health and appearance could also be normal goods, leading one to invest more time and money in slimness and fitness as income rises. The impact of income on weight may follow an inverted U-shape; the first few thousand dollars may be spent heavily on food and thus may increase weight, but at some point the marginal utility of food may decline to the point that additional income results in little increase in calorie consumption; instead, the additional income may be invested in weight loss for the sake of appearance and health (Philipson and Posner, 2003; Lakdawalla et al., 2005). 
Researchers have exploited natural experiments to study the impact of income on several relevant outcomes, including weight, calorie intake, and food expenditures. A variety of papers have examined the impact of income shocks on weight for specific groups. In general, they tend to find small or no effects of income on weight. For example, Schmeiser (2009) takes advantage of a significant expansion in the Earned Income Tax Credit during the 1990s and uses variation across states and over time in the generosity of the maximum EITC benefit as an instrument for income among families eligible for the EITC. He cannot reject the null hypothesis of no effect of income on weight for men, but finds a modest but statistically significant effect for women, for whom an additional $\$ 1000$ of income per year was associated with an increase in weight of roughly one pound. 
Akee et al. (2013) estimate the impact of income on adolescent weight using payments made by the Eastern Band of Cherokee 
Indians to their members in western North Carolina. Every six months, the tribe pays a share of casino profits to each member; the payments have averaged $\$ 4000$ per year, are tax free, and raise income by an average of $4 0 \%$ in households with two tribal members. The authors use two control groups: a pure control group of nearby non-tribal members, and a partial control group of adolescent tribal members whose households received the benefit for only two years instead of six during adolescence (the payments began in 1996). The authors find evidence of the non-linearity of weight in income; the payments increase BMI among adolescents from low-income households but have no impact on weight for adolescents in higher-income households. 
Cawley et al. (2010) estimate the effect of income on weight among the elderly, exploiting the natural experiment of the Social Security Benefits Notch, which was a legislative accident that created substantial unanticipated increases in retirement benefits (averaging roughly $\$ 1000$ in each year of retirement) for certain birth year cohorts in the United States. The authors find no statistically significant impact of income on BMI or the probability of obesity for either men or women, and for men they find precisely estimated zero effects. 
In general, these papers measure a Local Average Treatment Effect among lower-income individuals. Neither the Social Security payments examined by Cawley et al. (2010) nor the casino payments examined by Akee et al. (2013) were means tested, but the affected groups were relatively lower-income; Social Security beneficiaries tend to have lower earned income (though greater wealth) than working-age adults and Native Americans have a lower median income and higher poverty rate than the nation as a whole (U.S. Census Bureau, 2011). The EITC recipients examined by Schmeiser (2009) are unambiguously a low-income group. 
Weight may be especially responsive to income among lowincome individuals in developing countries. In a conditional income transfer program in Mexico, families spent an average of $7 0 \%$ of the cash transfer on what the authors describe as “better quality calories”: meat, fruits, and vegetables (Fernald et al., 2008a). The estimated impact of the income transfers on weight (measured using variation in payments due to randomized year of program incorporation but also on family demographic structure which may bias the results) differed for adults and children. For adults, a doubling of payments raised the risk of obesity in adults by $4 1 \%$ (Fernald et al., 2008a), whereas for children aged 24–68 months, it lowered the prevalence of overweight by $8 \%$ (Fernald et al., 2008b). The authors are not able to explain this age difference in the income effect, and are not able to observe mechanisms for the effects on weight. 
Evidence based on natural experiments implies that the impact of income on food expenditures in developed countries is very small.13 Parker et al. (2008) exploit the timing and amount of stimulus payments made to individuals by the U.S. government during the Great Recession and estimate that recipient households increased their expenditures on food by roughly $2 \%$ of the payment in the three months following the payment. Kuhn et al. (2011) examine the effects of winning the Dutch Postcode Lottery. Comparing participants in winning postcodes to participants in nearby non-winning postcodes, the authors find no significant impact of winning on the amount spent on food at home. Monthly expenditures on food away from home were significantly higher for the winners, but the authors note that this was also true a year before winning, so may be driven by unobserved heterogeneity. 
The literature on the cycle of purchases and consumption around monthly program receipt indicates that there are shortterm income effects on the quantity of food purchased and calories consumed among low-income individuals participating in government transfer programs. Shapiro (2005) finds that, among food stamp recipients, calorie consumption (estimated from dietary recall data) falls by $0 . 4 0 \%$ each day after receipt of food stamps, or by $1 0 { - } 1 5 \%$ over the course of the month. Hastings and Washington (2010) examine scanner data from three supermarkets in Nevada and estimate that the quantity of food purchased by households receiving food stamps or cash welfare benefits declines $3 2 \%$ throughout the month. Other households that are not benefit recipients do not exhibit the same decline during the month. Mastrobuoni and Weinberg (2009) observe similar behavior among Social Security recipients with low retirement savings; such individuals consume $2 4 \%$ fewer calories the week before receiving their Social Security check relative to the week afterward. In contrast, elderly with more retirement savings tend to smooth their consumption. Although this literature is specific to the cyclical nature of calorie intake in response to monthly payments, it suggests that, for low-income individuals, additional income may lead to higher calorie consumption, at least in the short run. Future research should seek to measure the effects of these cycles of consumption on weight, both within the month (which should be modest) and the cumulative effect over time. 
Cumulatively, the evidence suggests that, among very lowincome individuals in developed countries, additional income leads to greater expenditures on food, higher calorie intake, and slightly higher weight among adults. Among more middle-income individuals in developed countries (and even the average social security recipient), there is generally no detectable impact of income on weight. There is a need for studies of the impact of income on weight for higher-income individuals. 
2.4. Education
One of the most consistent findings in health economics is that those with higher education tend to be in better health (see, e.g. Grossman, 2006), and obesity is no exception. In the NHANES data for 2005–2008, the prevalence of adult obesity is $2 3 . 4 \%$ for women with a college degree and $4 2 . 1 \%$ for women who are high school dropouts (Ogden et al., 2010). For men, the correlation is weaker; the prevalence of obesity is $2 7 . 4 \%$ for men with a college degree compared to $3 2 . 1 \%$ for men who are high school dropouts (Ibid). 
Increased schooling may promote healthy weight later in life through a variety of mechanisms, such as greater health information, healthier diets, increased recreational physical activity, higher income, better health insurance, healthier peers, and safer residential neighborhoods that are more conducive to outdoor exercise. On the other hand, occupations that require higher education may be more sedentary. A large number of studies have estimated the impact of education on health in general and weight in particular; see reviews by Lochner (2011) and Cutler and Lleras-Muney (2010). 
A series of papers have exploited changes in compulsory schooling laws as an instrument for number of years of completed schooling. One of the strongest such papers is Clark and Royer (2013), which exploits a particularly powerful natural experiment: two changes to British compulsory schooling laws that increased minimum school leaving age from 14 to 16, which affected a majority of the relevant age cohort. Using a regression discontinuity approach that exploits the fact that these changes applied to specific month-year birth cohorts but not others, the authors cannot reject the null hypothesis that schooling has no impact on BMI, overweight, or obesity, although in some cases the estimates are substantial (and positive) but imprecise. 
Brunello et al. (2013) exploit changes in compulsory schooling laws during the 1960s and 1970s that raised the minimum school-leaving age by 1–3 years, pooling data from several European countries (9 for females and 7 for males). The IV models indicate that they cannot reject the null hypothesis of no impact of schooling on BMI for men. However, the IV models for women suggest that years of schooling lowers BMI of females; an increase in schooling of $1 0 \%$ (slightly more than 1 additional year) reduces women’s average BMI by $1 . 8 4 \%$ and the prevalence of obesity by $1 . 8 5 \%$ points $( 1 4 . 8 3 \% )$ . 
A team of researchers has exploited a series of natural experiments in the German educational system after World War II, and estimated their impact on weight using recent German Microcensus data (which unfortunately do not include highest grade completed but instead the secondary school track completed, from which the authors impute years of schooling). Models estimated using these various instruments all indicate that there is no detectable impact of education on weight for women, but the results for men differ considerably by instrument. Kemptner et al. (2011) uses changes in compulsory schooling laws as an instrument and finds that, for men, an additional year of schooling lowers BMI by 0.3 units and lowers the risk of obesity by $3 . 0 \%$ points. Jürges et al. (2011) use the construction of academically demanding elementary schools as an instrument. For men, the IV model indicates that an additional year of schooling raises the probability of overweight by $1 2 . 3 \%$ points and raises the probability of obesity by $1 3 . 3 \%$ points; both are implausibly large (rather than describing this as evidence that education increases weight, the authors more cautiously describe it as an absence of evidence that education decreases weight). The third paper in this series (Reinhold and Jürges, 2010) uses abolition of secondary school fees as an instrument and cannot reject the null hypothesis of no effect of education on overweight or obesity for men or women. 
Anderson and Matsa (2011) examines the effect of an additional year of schooling on weight, but what differentiates this paper from the previous ones is that the outcome is weight at the time that the subjects are still in early elementary school instead of weight during adulthood, when the subjects have completed their schooling. The authors estimate regression discontinuity models that exploit the child’s birth date relative to the cutoff date for school entry, and find no significant effect on BMI or the probability of obesity of being a relatively young first-grader instead of a relatively old kindergartener at the same age. 
There has been considerable interest in the promise of early childhood interventions to improve adult outcomes (e.g. Heckman et al., 2013). An evaluation of one well-known early childhood intervention, the High/Scope Perry Preschool Program, measured the impact of being randomized to the treatment (a two-year program of $2 . 5 \mathrm { h }$ of academic instruction daily) at age 3–4 years on obesity at age 40 (Muennig et al., 2009). It found that the Perry Preschool treatment had no measurable effect on the probability of adult obesity; in fact, the treatment and control groups had an identical obesity prevalence of $2 7 \%$ . An important caveat is that the sample is extremely small $( N = 1 0 3$ ). 
Another well-known early childhood intervention is the Carolina Abecedarian Project, which consisted of two stages: an early-childhood stage (from birth to age 5) that sought to facilitate development of language and cognitive skills, and a school-age stage (from age 6 through 8 years) that sought to improve math and reading skills. Campbell et al. (2014) found no evidence of any treatment effect on health from the second, school-age stage, and found mixed results for how the first early-childhood stage may have affected obesity. 
Assignment to the treatment group in the first stage was associated with a substantial reduction in the probability of being overweight throughout childhood for boys, but did not have a significant impact on the probability of being overweight for girls (Campbell et al., 2014). When subjects were in their mid-30s, data was collected from physical examinations that included measuring weight, height, and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR). Point estimates suggested that the treatment was associated with a large reduction in the probability of obesity (defined by either BMI or WHR) for males, but these were not statistically significant. For females, the treatment was associated with a $1 9 . 8 \%$ point $( 2 6 \% )$ lower probability of obesity defined by WHR, which was statistically significant. Oddly, the treatment was associated with a higher probability of obesity defined by BMI, but this was not statistically significant. The estimates tend to be imprecise because the sample is very small; follow-up physical examination data could be collected for only 68 subjects, and that is before dividing the sample by sex. Another important caveat is that both the Perry Preschool Program and Abecedarian treatments had multiple components; Perry Preschool included home visits by teachers, and Abecedarian included health care and nutritional components. Thus, the estimated effects reflect not just the effect of classroom education but the other program elements as well. 
Two studies have examined the health impact of the Head Start program and concluded that it reduces the risk of youth obesity. Carneiro and Ginja (2014) estimate a fuzzy regression discontinuity model that exploits a number of discontinuities across family size, family structure, state, and year in income eligibility for Head Start. Examining data on boys in the Children of the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth, 1979 Cohort, they find that participating in Head Start between the ages of 3 and 5 years reduces the probability of obesity at ages 12–13 by $2 9 \%$ and has no impact on the probability of obesity at ages 16–17. The effect at ages 12–13 seems to be concentrated among African-American boys. (The authors cannot estimate the impact of the program for girls because there is no jump in the probability of Head Start participation for girls around the income cutoffs.) 
Frisvold and Lumeng (2011) identify the impact of full-day versus half-day Head Start for youth, exploiting the natural experiment of an unanticipated cancelation, due to statewide budget cuts to education, of a grant that funded full-day Head Start. They examine five years of data from one Head Start program in Michigan that include multiple measurements of weight and height for each child $( N = 1 5 3 2 )$ . Their IV estimates indicate that full-day (compared to half-day) Head Start decreases youth obesity by $1 7 . 6 \%$ points. This may be implausibly large, but Hausman tests indicate that their non-IV model is preferred; that model implies a $4 \%$ point reduction in obesity, which the authors estimate could be explained by a reduction in consumption of 20 calories per day. 
The results of this research are extremely varied. Overall, it seems that early childhood education (including Head Start) may have protective effects at younger but not necessarily older ages, and that primary and secondary schooling has little if any impact on obesity. Future research should examine whether the overall null effect found in some studies is an average of meaningfully different effects in different subgroups; Cutler et al. (forthcoming) find that the association of education with obesity varies considerably across countries (see Fig. 1D in that paper), which suggests that it may also vary by region, race, and ethnicity. All of the studies discussed in this section measure the effect of preschool, primary or secondary schooling; future research should seek to identify the effect of additional years of college education. 
2.5. Macroeconomic conditions
The macroeconomy is another possible contributor to obesity. When the unemployment rate is high many people experience declines in income and employment. This decrease in labor market opportunities can have both substitution and income effects. The substitution effect is relatively straightforward: when wages are low so is the opportunity cost of time, and thus recessions may lead to more time-intensive investments in health such as physical activity and cooking from scratch. The income effect on health is ambiguous. Lower income could lead to a decrease in calorie consumption (e.g. cooking rice and beans at home instead of buying fast food) or an increase in calories (e.g. substituting energy-dense, low nutrient foods for more expensive fresh fruits and vegetables). Even conditional on income and employment, economic downturns could affect health by increasing stress, taking up mental bandwidth and reducing self-control or willpower (Mani et al., 2013), with deleterious consequences for diet and physical activity (e.g. De Vriendt et al., 2009). Stress also leads to the release of the hormone cortisol, which has complex effects but can increase appetite (especially for energy-dense foods), insulin resistance, and increased abdominal fat accumulation (e.g. Bjorntorp, 1997). 
Ruhm (2005) tested whether the state employment rate was associated with excess weight and physical inactivity. Reducedform models were estimated under the assumption that changes in local unemployment rates are exogenous; the models control for state, month, and year fixed effects, and thus identification comes from variation in the employment rate within states over time, relative to the corresponding changes in other states. Results of these models, estimated using data from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) for 1987–2000, are consistent with Ruhm’s general findings that “good times make you sick” and there is “healthy living in hard times.” In models that exclude employment status and income (and thus allow the effect of the employment rate to operate through changes in those variables), a $1 \%$ point decrease in the employment rate is estimated to reduce the probability of obesity by $0 . 7 3 \%$ points and to decrease the probability of physical inactivity by $0 . 2 1 \%$ points. These results are driven by improvements among the least healthy; morbid obesity and complete inactivity decrease. The results do not operate through changes in employment; in fact, the results are of equal size or larger for the employed population. In earlier work, Ruhm (2000) examined the BRFSS for 1987–1995 and found that a $1 \%$ point increase in the state unemployment rate was associated with a $2 \%$ decrease in daily fat intake, but no statistically significant change in daily servings of fruits and vegetables. Ruhm (2005) cautions that these results are specific to temporary macroeconomic fluctuations and do not imply that long-run economic growth worsens weight and exercise. 
In contrast to Ruhm’s findings that weight-related health is counter-cyclical, Charles and DeCicca (2008) find evidence that it is procyclical, with this relationship concentrated among the economically vulnerable. Because they examine MSA-level unemployment rates instead of state-level employment rates, they have to exclude those living outside of large MSAs from their sample of men in the National Health Interview Survey for 1997–2001. Estimating models by race, they find that a $1 \%$ point increase in the unemployment rate leads to a $2 . 2 \%$ point increase in the probability of obesity for African-American men, with smaller and statistically insignificant changes in the probability of obesity for Hispanic and White men. Estimating models based on the predicted probability that the respondent was employed, they find that those least likely to be employed experience the greatest increases in weight when the unemployment rate rises. They explore possible mechanisms and find no consistent link between the unemployment rate and exercise. Noting that their results are concentrated among low-SES individuals who may be at greatest risk from macroeconomic downturns, they hypothesize that their results are due to the biological effects of stress, such as stress-related eating, and elevated levels of cortisol promoting the accumulation of body fat. Other evidence on mechanisms comes from Dave and Kelly (2012), who find robust evidence in the BRFSS for 1990–2009 that a higher unemployment rate is associated with reduced consumption of fruits and vegetables, with weaker evidence of substitution toward snacks and fast food. 
Arkes (2009) studies the impact of the macroeconomy on the weight of young adults (aged 15–18 years) in the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth, 1997 Cohort, for 1997–2004. Arkes (2009) estimates models similar to those of Ruhm (2000) and finds that male teenagers gain weight in strong macroeconomic times, but female teenagers do the opposite – they gain weight during macroeconomic downturns. A $1 \%$ point increase in the state unemployment rate is associated with a $1 . 3 \%$ point lower probability that a teenage boy is overweight and a $1 . 8 \%$ point higher probability that a teenage girl is overweight.14 
One important mechanism by which macroeconomic conditions may affect the weight of adults is through job loss. Deb et al. (2011) identify the impact of job loss using respondent reports of whether they lost their job because the business closed; the rationale is that a firm shutdown is more exogenously determined than the job separation of individuals. They utilize data on middle-aged and elderly Americans in the Health and Retirement Study, 1992–2008. Speculating that there may be important heterogeneity in people’s response to job loss, they estimate a finite mixture model. They find that the first latent component $84 \%$ of the sample) had small but statistically insignificant decreases in BMI, but the second latent component ( $1 6 \%$ of the sample) had large and statistically significant increases in BMI, after business closure. In this second group, job loss resulted in a gain of one BMI unit, or roughly 7 pounds for a man of average height. People in this second, more affected, component differed from those in the first in that they were $2 2 \%$ more likely to be obese, and were more likely to be female, lower-income, less educated, and middle-aged rather than elderly. These results are consistent with those of Charles and DeCicca (2008); the economically vulnerable appear to be most affected by the macroeconomy and job loss. 
In summary, there appears to be considerable heterogeneity across age, gender, urbanicity, and socioeconomic status in the effect of the macroeconomy on diet, physical activity, and weight. It is unclear the extent to which these differing results are a result of studies using different methods and datasets and examining different time periods; future research could seek to estimate consistent models in a single dataset for various subgroups. 
Although the results are quite divergent, the fact that the unemployment rate has repeatedly been associated with diet, physical activity, and weight should make researchers cautious about using unemployment rate as an instrument for other possible causes of obesity. For example, unemployment rate has been used as an instrument for maternal employment in a model of child obesity (e.g. Anderson et al., 2003), but this may be invalid because the unemployment rate may have a direct impact on child weight and thus be correlated with the second-stage error term in the IV model. 
2.6. Peer and neighborhood effects
Peers may influence diet or physical activity through bandwagon effects; it may be more enjoyable to eat, diet, or exercise when one’s peers are doing the same. Identifying peer effects is notoriously difficult because individuals tend to associate with people like themselves and peers tend to have similar environmental influences (Manski, 1993). Yakusheva et al. (2014) identify peer effects by exploiting the randomization of freshman roommates at two universities. Following the literature that defines the roommate receiving influence as the ego and the roommate doing the influencing as the alter, they estimate the influence of the alter’s weight at baseline (just before freshman year) on the change in weight of the ego during freshman year; the model also controls for a complete set of indicator variables for all possible combinations of the factors used by the administration to make roommate assignments (such as preferred area of campus and smoking status). Results from these models are consistent with a significant positive peer effect on weight for females; for every standard deviation increase in the baseline weight of the alter, the ego gains an additional 0.8 pounds during freshman year (compared to an average freshman year weight gain of 2.4 pounds). They cannot reject the null hypothesis of no peer effects on weight for males. The authors explore heterogeneity in these peer effects, and find that the females who are most influenced by their roommates are those in the lowest and highest quartiles of the baseline weight distribution. They also find that alters have the greatest effect on egos when the alter is higher in the social hierarchy; specifically, when the alter is lighter, comes from a higher-SES household, or is more sexually experienced than the ego. There are no such differences for males. 
A major social experiment found strong evidence of neighborhood effects, which may in part reflect the impact of a new set of peers. Kling et al. (2007) and Ludwig et al. (2011) examine the effect of the Moving to Opportunity program on weight. From 1994 to 1998, the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development randomized nearly 4500 women with children who were living in public housing in high-poverty urban census tracts in five major U.S. cities to one of three groups: one received housing vouchers that were redeemable only if they moved to a low-poverty census tract, a second received the unrestricted traditional Section 8 housing vouchers and a third received neither of these opportunities. 
In 2002, an average of 5 years after random assignment, the researchers collected data on self-reported weight. For adults, the effect of Intention to Treat was that the group offered the lowpoverty vouchers was $4 . 8 \%$ points less likely to be obese than the control group; assuming that there was no effect on those who did not use the voucher to move, the effect of Treatment on the Treated was a $1 0 . 3 \%$ point reduction in the probability of obesity (Kling et al., 2007). 
The researchers conducted another follow-up in 2008–2010, an average of 12.6 years after randomization, and this time measured the respondents’ weight, height, and tested their blood for diabetes. Ludwig et al. (2011) estimate that the effect of Intention to Treat was that the group offered low-poverty vouchers were $4 . 6 1 \%$ points $( 1 3 . 0 \% )$ less likely to have a $\mathsf { B M I } \geq 3 5 , 3 . 3 8 \%$ points $( 1 9 . 1 \% )$ less likely to have a ${ \mathrm { B M I } } \geq 4 0$ , and were $4 . 3 1 \%$ points $( 2 1 . 6 \% )$ less likely to have diabetes. The small difference in the probability of overall obesity $\left( \mathsf { B M I } \geq 3 0 \right)$ was not statistically significant. The group randomized to the traditional Section 8 voucher program was less likely than the control group to have a $\mathsf { B M I } \geq 3 5$ but the other differences between the Section 8 treatment group and the control group were not statistically significant. Thus, it seems that the requirement to move to a low-income neighborhood was important for the beneficial effect on weight. 
Some of these studies were able to investigate the mechanisms by which these peer and neighborhood effects operate. Kling et al. (2007) attribute the lower rate of obesity among movers to increases in exercise and improvements in nutrition (which could be the result of new peers or a lower time cost of shopping for healthy food). Yakusheva et al. (2014) test for peer effects on self-reported exercise frequency and eating disorders but find little evidence of any. Carrell et al. (2011) exploits the randomized assignment of students at the U.S. Air Force Academy to squadrons (groups of 30 with whom cadets spend most of their time) to test whether being assigned to a squadron of alters who were less fit during high school affects two important outcomes: the cadet’s physical education score that semester and whether the cadet failed the Academy’s physical fitness requirements. Using a sample that is unusually large for this topic (over 13,000), they regress the ego’s outcomes on the average high school fitness scores of the rest of the squadron (the alters). The results indicate that a one standard deviation increase in the high school fitness scores of all peers in the group raises the ego’s college fitness score by 0.165 of a standard deviation; this is large – roughly $4 0 \%$ as strong as the effect of the ego’s own high school fitness. A one standard deviation increase in the high school fitness scores of all peers in the group also lowers the probability that the ego fails the college fitness test by $4 . 7 \%$ points; this is even larger, approximately $7 0 \%$ as strong as the effect of the ego’s own high school fitness. In other models they find that the most influential alters, and the most influenced egos, are those who are least fit. However, before generalizing from this finding one should keep in mind that cadets at the U.S. Air Force Academy are specifically selected to be fit, so the least fit in that group are still above the population average. 
Overall, there is evidence of peer and neighborhood effects on weight for women and on fitness for men. An important priority is to better explain this gender differences in peer effects, identify peer effects for other peer groups of interest, such as coworkers, and how it varies over the life cycle (e.g. do peer effects on weight among women peak during the teenage years?). It would be useful to know whether eating disorders such as anorexia nervosa and bulimia can spread through female networks in high school and college (such as sororities and sports and cheerleading teams), although this likely faces the additional challenges of a high degree of misreporting and a relatively uncommon outcome. It is also a priority to determine how to harness the positive influence of peer effects to increase retention and improve outcomes in exercise, diet and weight loss programs. 
3. The economic consequences of obesity
The economic consequences of obesity include worse labor market outcomes, such as lower wages and a lower probability of employment, and higher medical care costs. When studying the causes of obesity, there is often the potential to conduct randomized experiments: e.g. to randomize children into a preschool program in order to measure the impact of education on obesity or to randomize college roommates in order to test for peer effects in weight. That is more difficult when obesity is the endogenous regressor rather than the outcome. It would, obviously, be unethical to force a subject randomized to the treatment group to become obese so one could study the resulting increase in medical care costs. It is possible to randomly assign overweight individuals interested in weight loss to a weight loss treatment, but the effect measured would reflect not just the weight change but also the method of achieving it (such as bariatric surgery or increased time spent physically active) which could have a direct impact on the outcome. Due to the challenges of experimentally manipulating individuals’ weights, the literature on the economic consequences of obesity has relied solely on natural experiments for identification. 
In contrast to the research on the economic causes of obesity, which was often characterized by conflicting results, the evidence on the economic consequences of obesity is relatively consistent: obesity worsens labor market outcomes and raises medical care costs. One interesting aspect of this literature is that the medical definition of obesity (a BMI of 30 or higher) does not coincide with the economic consequences of obesity; wages for women begin falling with BMI in the healthy weight range (Kline and Tobias, 2008; Gregory and Ruhm, 2011) while medical care costs do not rise significantly with BMI until the morbidly obese range (Cawley et al., 2015). 
3.1. Wages
A large literature has estimated the effect of weight or obesity on wages (see Averett, 2011, for a review). Cawley (2004) exploits the heritable component of weight by using the BMI of an adult biological sibling as an instrument for the BMI of the respondent. This instrument has the advantage of being powerful; twin studies indicate very high levels of heritability for BMI, up to $7 0 \mathrm { - } 8 0 \%$ (Farooqi and O’Rahilly, 2007; Pietilainen, 2014). The potential threat to validity is that the genes that affect obesity may also affect other things correlated with the error term in the wage equation. One might also be concerned about the possibility that a shared household environment makes biological relatives similar in weight and could be correlated with outcomes, but the preponderance of evidence suggests that any shared household environment effect on weight is so small as to be undetectable (see the review in Cawley and Meyerhoefer, 2012); consistent with this, the weight of nonbiological relatives (e.g. stepchildren or adopted children) are not significant predictors of respondent weight in the first stage of IV (Cawley, 2004; Lindeboom et al., 2010; Cawley and Meyerhoefer, 2012). Estimating IV models using data from the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth, 1979 Cohort, for 1981–2000, Cawley (2004) finds that the effect of weight on wages varies by race and gender, with the greatest impact on white females, for whom an additional 10 pounds lowers wages by $2 . 8 \%$ . 
Several other studies have applied this IV method to samples for other countries. For example, Brunello and D’Hombres (2007) apply the method to data for nine countries in the European Community Household Panel; their IV models indicate that weight lowers wages for women in Italy, Portugal and Finland, and for men in Italy, Spain, Austria, and Ireland; some of the null results may be due to the very small samples in each country (485–5355). When they pool the data on all nine countries, their IV models indicate that a $1 0 \%$ increase in BMI reduces the wages of females by $1 . 8 6 \%$ and males by $3 . 2 7 \%$ . 
To test for non-linearities in these effects, Kline and Tobias (2008) allow BMI to enter the IV wage equation non-parametrically and instrument for respondent weight using the weight of a biological parent. Using data from the 1970 British Cohort Study, they estimate that BMI lowers the wages of women along the entire range of BMI, but the decrease is steepest in the range of approximately BMI 20–28. They also find that BMI lowers wages for men, and in a relatively linear fashion. 
In summary, there is evidence that weight lowers wages in economically developed countries. Studies have found this effect for both women and men, but the magnitude of the effects differs by gender and race. For example, in the U.S. the obesity wage penalty is greater for women than men and greater for white females than African-American or Hispanic females (Cawley, 2004). Future research should examine the extent to which these wage gaps are due to discrimination, job absenteeism and lower productivity on the job; I return to this topic in the later section on the externalities associated with obesity. 
3.2. Employment
Others have examined the effect of obesity on employment. Lindeboom et al. (2010) examine data from the British National Child Development Study, which consists of 17,000 individuals born in Great Britain in one week in 1958, and records employment at ages 33 and 42 years. They observe large differences in the probability of employment between obese and non-obese men and women, but when they estimate IV models that use parental obesity status as an instrument for respondent weight, they cannot reject the null hypothesis that obesity has no effect on employment. Another study to test for an impact of obesity on employment in the UK (Morris, 2007) instrumented for respondent obesity using the prevalence of obesity in the local health authority, but this may be invalid if respondents sort into neighborhoods based on unobserved demand (e.g. for proximity to restaurants or exercise opportunities). 
Although it is difficult to experimentally manipulate individuals’ weights, one clever study devised a way to experimentally manipulate employer’s perceptions of job applicants’ weights. Rooth (2009) tested for discrimination against obese job applicants by submitting fictitious job applications to 989 real job openings in urban Sweden. The applications were sent in pairs, with resumes created to be as identical as possible without arousing employer suspicion. Each of the applications included a photo of the applicant, which is a common practice in Sweden. The paired applicants were chosen to be as identical as possible (with respect to hair color, hair length, smile, and overall attractiveness), but the photo for one of the two was manipulated using computer software to make the individual appear obese. The study estimates that the obese applicant was significantly less likely to be contacted for a job interview than the other member of the pair; this disadvantage equaled $8 \%$ points for women and $6 \%$ points for men. The disadvantage was similar across occupation for men, but it varied across occupation for women: $2 1 \%$ points for those applying to be restaurant workers, $1 5 \%$ points for those applying to be accountants and $7 \%$ points for those applying to be preschool teachers, but no significant difference for applications to be computer specialists or nurses. When the author controls for a third-party rating of the attractiveness of the photograph, he finds that employer discrimination is on the basis of obesity for female applicants and on the basis of attractiveness for male applicants. A large number of laboratory studies that ask subjects to make hypothetical decisions about hiring, compensation and promotions also find consistent evidence of discrimination against obese workers and applicants (see, e.g. Roehling et al., 2013). 
3.3. Medical care costs
Given the strong link between obesity and heart disease, cancer, and Type II diabetes, another likely economic consequence of obesity is elevated medical care costs. Cawley and Meyerhoefer (2012) and Cawley et al. (2015) estimate IV two-part models of medical expenditures using data from the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey. The IV model exploits the heritable component of weight by instrumenting for respondent obesity using the BMI and BMI squared of a biological child. The models indicate that in 2010 obesity raised annual medical care costs by $\$ 3508$ (in 2010 dollars); however, the effects of BMI on medical care costs are highly non-linear (Cawley et al., 2015). For men, medical care costs are Ushaped in BMI, with much higher expenditures for those who are underweight or morbidly obese. For women, medical care costs are J-shaped in BMI with a large increase in medical care costs with BMI among those who are morbidly obese. For both men and women, predicted medical care costs for individuals just over the threshold of obesity are not significantly higher than those of healthy weight individuals. However, costs rise exponentially in the morbidly obese range $\left( \mathsf { B M I } \ge 3 5 \right)$ , which indicates that the greatest potential savings may come from facilitating weight loss in that group or preventing additional weight gain among obese individuals. This pattern of medical care expenditures matches that of mortality risk: flat throughout the overweight and obese class 1 range, rising with BMI in the morbidly obese range; see Flegal et al. (2013). Assuming that the estimates from the regression sample of adults with biological children in the household generalize to the full population of non-institutionalized adults, the IV model implies that the total direct medical care costs of obesity for the U.S. population of adults was $\$ 315.8$ billion in 2010, which equaled $2 7 . 5 \%$ of the health expenditures for non-institutionalized U.S. adults (Cawley et al., 2015). 
4. The negative externalities of obesity
Obesity imposes large external costs, which likely decrease social welfare. Third-party payers cover most of the costs of obesity related illness; e.g. in 2005, $8 8 . 2 \%$ of the total medical care costs of obesity in the U.S. were paid by health insurance companies, Medicare, and Medicaid (Cawley and Meyerhoefer, 2012). Ultimately, many of these costs of obesity were paid by non-obese individuals. In group health insurance, the premium paid by each individual does not depend upon his or her weight; as a result, healthy-weight individuals subsidize the health care costs of obese individuals. The same is true for Medicare, which is funded by payroll taxes, and Medicaid, which is funded with general revenues; in public health insurance programs, healthy-weight individuals subsidize the medical care costs of obese individuals. 
No one has challenged that obesity imposes negative externalities through public health insurance. However, the extent of externalities in employer-provided group health insurance has been questioned; Bhattacharya and Bundorf (2009) argue that obese workers with employer-provided health insurance pay for their higher health care costs in the form of lower wages. However, if wage penalties existed to internalize medical care costs, then they should be negligible until the morbidly obese range (Cawley et al., 2015). In fact, women’s wages fall with BMI along the entire range of BMI and the marginal effect of BMI on wages is largest in the healthy weight range (Kline and Tobias, 2008), where medical care costs are flat over BMI (see Cawley and Meyerhoefer, 2012, Fig. 3). Gregory and Ruhm (2011) estimate flexible IV models of weight and wages in which sibling BMI is an instrument for the BMI of the respondent, using the Panel Survey of Income Dynamics, and conclude that women’s wages peak at a BMI of roughly 23, far below the threshold of obesity or even overweight, which they interpret as evidence against the theory of Bhattacharya and Bundorf (2009); instead, they conclude that wages fall with BMI because employers have preferences for appearance. A large number of studies document employer discrimination on the basis of weight; see Puhl (2011) for a review. 
In addition, obese individuals earn less in many countries of the world that have universal public health insurance, such as Austria, Denmark, England, Finland, France, Germany, Italy, and Sweden (see e.g. Caliendo and Gehrsitz, 2014; Averett, 2011; Brunello and D’Hombres, 2007). In such countries employers do not provide health insurance and thus they have no incentive or even ability to internalize the external medical care costs of obesity. Overall, it seems more likely that the obesity wage penalty is due to employer discrimination or lower productivity than an attempt by employers to internalize the external health care costs of obesity. 
Health care cost externalities of obesity can lead to deadweight loss in two ways (Bhattacharya and Sood, 2011, 2007). First, externalities raise average premiums in the private health insurance market, which results in lower take-up and suboptimal amounts of coverage.15 In public health insurance, the higher medical care costs are passed on to the public in the form of higher payroll taxes (for Medicare) and income taxes (for Medicaid), which leads to decreased labor supply.16 Thus, the external costs of obesity in health insurance distort decisions in both health insurance markets and labor markets, imposing deadweight loss. 
The second potential source of deadweight loss arises because of moral hazard; individuals who do not pay the full medical care costs of excess weight may underinvest in a healthy diet, physical activity, weight loss or prevention of weight gain (Bhattacharya and Sood, 2007). Several studies have examined the effects of increasing health insurance coverage at the extensive or intensive margin, but it is difficult to infer much from this about moral hazard because increased insurance, by increasing consumption of health care, may have a direct effect on BMI. 
Several studies examine the extensive margin of health insurance. Dave and Kaestner (2009) examine changes in health behaviors among those who newly qualify for Medicare and find evidence consistent with moral hazard: controlling for employment status and number of doctor visits, gaining Medicare coverage is associated with a $1 5 . 1 \%$ point $( 4 0 \% )$ decrease in the probability that men engage in vigorous physical exercise. Other studies examine the impact on obesity as opposed to diet and physical activity. A randomized experiment found that providing Medicaid to Oregon residents had no statistically significant impact on the probability of obesity, although the confidence intervals were very wide (Baicker et al., 2013). In contrast, two studies that exploit state Medicaid expansions as natural experiments find evidence that health insurance coverage raises BMI (Kelly and Markowitz, 2009/2010; Bhattacharya et al., 2011). 
With respect to the intensive margin of insurance, Bhattacharya et al. (2011) examined historic data from the RAND health insurance experiment and found no evidence that the generosity of insurance raises BMI. 
Overall, the health insurance externality of obesity is likely to impose deadweight loss by reducing purchases of health insurance and reducing labor supply, and less likely to greatly distort decisions about diet, physical activity, and weight loss. Calculating the extent of such deadweight loss would be a useful direction for future research. 
5. Policies to internalize the external costs of obesity
There are several possible approaches to internalizing the external costs of obesity. The most direct would be to tax excess body fat by an amount equal to its marginal external cost. No country has yet implemented such a tax, perhaps because of concerns about discriminating against those with a genetic predisposition to fatness. However, Singapore implemented a program called “Trim and Fit” from 1992 to 2008 in which schoolchildren’s BMIs were measured and youths deemed overweight were assigned extra physical activity at school. There were no financial penalties for youths or their families but schools could be rewarded or punished based on progress toward goals. Singapore’s health ministry reported that the prevalence of youth obesity declined as a result (Toh et al., 2002) but it is not clear whether the intervention was rigorously evaluated. 
Japan in 2008 passed what was nicknamed the ‘metabo law’ (after the metabolic syndrome, a set of risk factors for heart disease that includes a large waist circumference) that requires companies and local governments to add to the mandatory checkups for adults aged 40–75 a measurement of waist circumference; individuals who exceed the limits (33.5 in. for men and 35.4 in. for women) are counseled on dieting and weight loss. There are no financial penalties for individuals but employers and local governments face fines for failing to meet targets. 
Most policies and programs that could be used to internalize the external costs of obesity do not levy taxes or penalties directly on fatness or weight. Instead, they take the form of: raising health insurance premia for obese individuals, offering financial rewards for weight loss, taxing foods that are energy-dense, subsidizing foods that are nutritious and less energy dense, and subsidizing physical activity. These programs are discussed in the following sections. 
5.3. Taxes on energy-dense foods
Financial rewards for weight loss have a mixed record of effectiveness (Cawley and Price, 2013; Department of the Treasury, Department of Labor, and Department of Health and Human Services, 2013; Horwitz et al., 2013; Jeffery, 2012). Some studies have found substantial weight loss in such programs. For example, Volpp et al. (2008) find that offering a lottery with an expected daily value of \$3 results in an average weight loss of 9.2 pounds relative to the control group after 16 weeks. In contrast, several studies have found no detectable weight loss from rewards that consisted of: $\$ 7-14$ per percentage point of weight lost (Finkelstein et al., 2007), $\$ 100$ per month for achieving weight loss goals (Kullgren et al., 2013), or $\$ 3-5$ per quarter for each percentage point of baseline weight lost (Cawley and Price, 2013). An important limitation of this research is that the participants are volunteers interested in weight loss; thus, it is unknown how effective incentives would be for people who do not wish to lose weight (Jeffery, 2012). Another complication is that attrition in such programs can be substantial, despite the fact that all participants are volunteers being paid to achieve their own goals; e.g. the workplace wellness program studied by Cawley and Price (2013) experienced attrition of $5 4 . 9 \%$ after 3 months and $7 5 . 8 \%$ after 12 months. 
Clearly, behavior change and attrition in such programs is partly a function of the way in which the incentives are designed (Volpp et al., 2011). In general, incentives are more effective when they are immediate, salient, and take advantage of loss aversion, mental accounting, and risk preferences (see, e.g. Ainslie, 1975; Volpp et al., 2011). An important research priority is to determine the optimal design of incentives for weight loss and related behavior change. 
Others have sought to incentivize exercise rather than weight. Royer et al. (forthcoming) conducted a randomized experiment at a Fortune 500 company in which the treatment group was offered $\$ 10$ per visit to the company gym for up to 3 visits per week for four weeks and find that the treatment doubled gym attendance. Charness and Gneezy (2009) find that offering university students $\$ 100$ to visit the gym at least eight times in four weeks more than doubles their weekly gym visits relative to a control group. Florida offered incentives for Medicaid enrollees to participate in exercise programs. Although the program was not formally evaluated, only two participants of the 428,000 who were automatically enrolled earned credits for participating in exercise programs; researchers have speculated that the incentives were too small, not salient, and that participants had insufficient knowledge of the program (Blumenthal et al., 2013). 
One concern about such incentive schemes is that the provision of external incentives may crowd out intrinsic motivation; the worst case scenario is that after the external incentives end, individuals are less motivated and health behaviors are worse than before the intervention. Studies of incentives for exercise find that exercise remains somewhat elevated after the removal of incentives (Royer et al., forthcoming; Charness and Gneezy, 2009). Likewise, studies have documented substantial weight regain after financial incentives for weight loss ended (e.g. Cawley and Price, 2013; Jeffery, 2012; John et al., 2011) but participants do not end up heavier than they were before the intervention. Thus, external incentives do not appear to crowd out intrinsic motivation for exercise or weight loss. 
Perhaps the anti-obesity policy that has been most commonly proposed (e.g. Kamerow, 2010; Brownell and Frieden, 2009; Brownell et al., 2009) and most heavily studied by economists is a tax on energy-dense, low-nutrient foods such as sugar-sweetened beverages (SSB). In the U.S., 34 states tax soft drinks sold in grocery stores, at an average rate of $4 . 0 2 \%$ , and 15 states tax snacks sold in grocery stores at an average rate of $1 . 2 \%$ (Chriqui et al., 2008). Numerous countries have recently taxed energy-dense, non-nutritious foods, such as Canada, Denmark, Finland, France, Hungary, Norway, and Mexico. 
Such taxes have practical challenges. It is unclear what set of foods should be taxed; there is no specific subset of foods that cause obesity, and the risk of taxing a small number of foods is that consumers may switch to energy-dense items that are untaxed (Chouinard et al., 2007; Fletcher et al., 2010a, 2013; Zhen et al., 2014). Another challenge is that when taxes are enacted by geographically small jurisdictions they can be evaded through cross-border shopping (Tosun and Skidmore, 2007). There is also concern that food taxes are regressive. However, several states both tax food and in general provide tax credits to low-income households, which allows for the substitution effect, but eliminates the income effect, of the tax (Cawley, 2012). 
Numerous studies have documented consumer responses to changes in food taxes. For example, Fletcher et al. (2010a,b) estimate models that control for state and year fixed effects using data on adults from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System for 1990–2006 (when 28 states changed their tax rates on SSBs), and on youths from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys for 1989–2006. For youths, they estimate that a $1 \%$ point increase in the tax on SSBs (relative to a mean of $2 . 7 \%$ ) decreases youth soft drink consumption by just 6 calories per day. However, that decreased consumption of SSBs is offset by increased consumption of milk, with the result that there is no significant change in total calories consumed. Not surprisingly, there is no detectable effect of SSB taxes on youth weight (Fletcher et al., 2010a). For adults, they find statistically significant but extremely small impacts of SSB taxes on weight; a $1 \%$ point (or nearly $5 0 \%$ ) increase in the state tax rate on SSBs leads to a decrease in BMI of 0.003 units and a decrease in obesity of $0 . 0 1 \%$ points (Fletcher et al., 2010b). 
A challenge for predicting the impact of large taxes on energydense foods is that they do not currently exist in the U.S. so it requires projecting out of sample. Some have sought to estimate the impact of larger taxes using elasticities of demand calculated using naturally occurring variation in food prices; a limitation of this is that the naturally occurring variation may be endogenous – due in part to differences in demand across areas or changes in demand over time within areas – resulting in biased estimates of elasticities. 
Finkelstein et al. (2013) simulate the effect of a $2 0 \%$ tax on SSBs, using Nielsen Homescan data and allowing for potential substitutions between SSBs and 19 categories of other types of food. They estimate two-stage residual inclusion IV models that address the endogeneity of prices by instrumenting for the household price index of each food category using the weighted average of the price indices of all other households in the same market excluding those in the same census tract; this assumes that all demand shocks are limited to the census tract, and are not common to the broader market. They estimate that a $2 0 \%$ price increase on SSBs would lead to very little substitution to other categories of energy-dense food and would reduce the purchases of calories from stores by $4 . 7 \%$ , which they estimate would result in a weight loss of 1.6 pounds in the first year and 2.9 pounds over 10 years. This paper examines 19 categories of food rather than the universe of groceries; a subsequent paper by the same authors that examines a broader set of foods that represented about half of all at-home caloric intake (and used a different functional form) found evidence of greater substitution away from SSBs and toward other food categories (Zhen et al., 2014). As a result, the estimated impact on weight of the $2 0 \%$ tax on SSBs was less: a reduction of 0.8 lbs for low-income individuals and 0.4 lbs for high-income individuals after one year, and a reduction of 1.5 lbs for low-income individuals and 0.7 lbs for high-income individuals after 10 years. Limitations of both studies are that they observe purchases, not consumption, they use data on half or less of calories consumed from groceries, and they do not observe food purchases from vending machines or restaurants. The model in Finkelstein et al. (2013) also does not allow for income effects from the taxes. 
A substantial number of studies have experimentally manipulated food prices, sometimes by very large amounts; however, these tend to lower the price of healthier foods rather than dramatically raise the price of energy-dense foods (Epstein et al., 2012). In general, it is difficult to experimentally simulate a tax in the field because people can avoid it by shopping elsewhere. 
Overall, the evidence suggests that taxes on energy-dense foods would probably have to be much higher than their current rates to have a meaningful impact on consumption (Fletcher et al., forthcoming, 2011; Chaloupka et al., 2011; Powell and Chaloupka, 2009), and even then if taxes are narrowly applied then substitution to untaxed items may result, which would limit the effect on weight (Fletcher et al., 2013; Schroeter et al., 2008; Chouinard et al., 2007; Zhen et al., 2014). 
5.4. Subsidies for healthy food
An alternative to taxing energy-dense, non-nutritious foods is to subsidize nutritious, less energy-dense foods. A large number of studies have experimentally subsidized the prices of healthy foods, sometimes by very large amounts; see the review in Epstein et al. (2012). Some take place in laboratory settings with questionable generalizability, but others have taken place in cafeterias or vending machines in the field. For example, subsidizing fruit and salads in university and high school cafeterias by $5 0 \%$ resulted in a tripling to quadrupling of sales (Jeffery et al., 1994; French et al., 1997); however, these price reductions were combined with other interventions such as signs and increased diversity of healthy food choices, so that increase in demand cannot be attributed solely to the price change. Moreover, the behavior change did not result in lasting healthier habits; sales fell back to baseline levels after the removal of the subsidy. 
More recently, Ni Mhurchu et al. (2010) conducted a randomized experiment to estimate the impact of a $1 2 . 5 \%$ discount on the price of healthier foods on grocery purchases. It was conducted in New Zealand with 1104 shoppers from one retail supermarket chain, and food and beverages were classified as healthier or less healthy using nutrition recommendations from the Heart Foundation. Purchases were tracked using supermarket electronic scanners. At six months, the impact of the discount on purchases of saturated fat, total fat, and sugars were small and statistically insignificant, but the treatment was associated with an $1 1 \%$ increase in purchases of healthier foods, which amounted to an additional $0 . 7 9 \mathrm { k g }$ per week, including $0 . 4 8 \mathrm { k g / w k }$ more fruit and vegetables. This may be an underestimate of the true price effect because the price change was not very salient: prices on supermarket shelves were not altered; instead, treatment group members were mailed a list of foods that were discounted. There is weak evidence that some of the increase in sales persisted 6 months after the discount ended. 
The USDA recently conducted a pilot test of a Healthy Incentives Program in SNAP that offered a $3 0 \%$ rebate on each dollar of SNAP benefits spent on targeted fruits and vegetables (they could be fresh, canned, frozen, or dried but without added sugar or fat; white potatoes and fruit juice were also excluded). The rebate was credited to the SNAP Electronic Benefit Transfer card for a maximum of $\$ 60$ per month, a cap that few households reached. The rebate could be spent on any SNAP-eligible groceries. A randomized controlled experiment involving 55,000 households indicated that the incentives increased consumption of fresh fruits and vegetables by $2 5 \%$ , which equaled 0.22 cups or roughly 3.5 tablespoons per day (USDA, 2013). 
While studies have demonstrated that subsidizing healthy foods can lead to increased purchases of those foods, they have not been shown to reduce obesity. That is, there is not yet evidence that the increased consumption of nutritious food leads to a decrease in the consumption of energy-dense, non-nutritious food sufficient to reduce weight. The risk is that such subsidies may increase overall calorie intake, further increasing weights. There may be health benefits from improved nutrition, however. 
5.5. Subsidies for physical activity
Taxpayers subsidize the physical activity of youth through sports teams and physical education (PE) classes in public schools. Several organizations, such as the Centers for Disease Control, American Academy of Pediatrics, and Institute of Medicine, have called for increases in PE to prevent and reduce youth obesity. To estimate the impact of PE on the physical activity and weight of high school students, Cawley et al. (2007) instrument for the amount of time that high school students spend in PE using state PE requirements, while controlling for state socioeconomic characteristics and education spending to reduce the risks of policy endogeneity. IV models, estimated using data from the Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System, indicate that increasing PE time increases physical activity among high school girls (but not boys) but has no detectable effect on BMI or the probability of obesity for either sex. 
A follow-up study (Cawley et al., 2013) estimates the impact of PE for younger children: those in elementary school. IV models that exploit variation across states in PE requirements and that are estimated using data from the Early Childhood Longitudinal Study, Kindergarten Class of 1998–1999 (ECLS-K), indicate that for children in kindergarten, 1st grade, or 3rd grade, PE has no detectable effect on weight. However, before the ECLS-K children reached 5th grade several populous states raised their PE requirements, and in the fifth grade wave the IV model indicates beneficial effects of PE; specifically, an additional $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ per week of PE reduces the probability of obesity by $4 . 8 \%$ points $( 2 2 \% )$ . This grade-specific result may be due to a difference in PE curriculum across grades, an age effect, the unusual number of state policy changes around that time, or No Child Left Behind leading to reductions in PE time for students in states without PE mandates. The result for 5th graders is concentrated among boys; parent-reported data on the youth participation in various activities suggest that PE is a complement with other physical activity for boys, but a substitute for other physical activity for girls. 
Overall, the results suggest that PE has no detectable effect on weight in most grades. However, it should be noted that this is the effect of the average PE curriculum; innovative PE programs that challenge and interest youth could be protective against obesity. Moreover, even if increased physical activity does not reduce weight, it has independent health benefits (IOM, 2012). 
7.3. Genetic markers
Genes are a particularly interesting natural experiment that epidemiologists refer to as Mendellian Randomization (Davey Smith and Ebrahim, 2005). Genes are an appealing source of variation because they are beyond the individual’s control and long precede the outcome – they are determined even before birth. However, to date it has proven challenging to use individual genes as instruments for obesity (Cawley et al., 2011). They tend not to be powerful instruments because, although genetic factors play an important role in obesity (Farooqi and O’Rahilly, 2007; Pietilainen, 2014) individual genes tend to have little independent influence on weight and geneticists’ understanding of those processes are far from complete; all known genetic polymorphisms combined explain less than $2 \%$ of the variance in obesity (Winter et al., 2013). There have also been challenges to validity; some studies have used as instruments alleles that, in addition to being only very weakly correlated with obesity, are associated with other risky behaviors and thus are likely to affect the outcome through channels other than obesity (see the discussion in Cawley et al., 2011). One of the strongest papers in this area is Von Hinke Kessler Scholder et al. (2012), which instruments for fat mass using the genetic marker FTO, which is highly correlated with fatness and does not seem to have the problems with validity that plagued genetic instruments used in earlier studies. As genetic research on obesity progresses, the suitability of specific genes as instruments in specific circumstances should become clearer. 
8. Conclusion
The large literature on the economics of obesity indicates that numerous economic variables, such as food prices, the time cost of food acquisition, income, education, the macroeconomy, and peer behaviors, may have modest effects on weight and obesity. The magnitude, and sometimes the sign, of these effects vary by subgroup (e.g. gender, race, age, and income). There appears to be no single dominant economic cause of obesity; instead, a wide variety of factors may each contribute a small amount, and different economic variables may be relevant for different groups. 
In contrast, the consequences of obesity are relatively clear: lower wages and higher medical care costs that impose negative externalities through health insurance. There exists an economic rationale for government intervention in order to internalize the negative externalities of obesity and provide information to consumers. Justifying government intervention on the grounds that consumers behave irrationally is contentious, and more widely accepted for children than adults. 
Research on economic approaches to treatment and prevention suggests that providing additional information through menu labels, taxing energy-dense foods, and offering financial rewards for weight loss, while justifiable as methods of addressing market failure, may have only modest effects on weight. Thus, just as there is no single cause of obesity there is no magic bullet that will reduce obesity; instead, a wide range of policies may be needed to have a substantial effect on the prevalence of obesity. An important caveat is that considerable research remains to be done to determine ways of making information, taxes, and financial rewards more effective in changing behavior. The large number of important research questions that remain suggests that the economics of obesity will continue to be an active and fruitful research area for many years to come.
