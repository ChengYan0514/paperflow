The Effects of Paid Maternity Leave: Evidence from Temporary Disability Insurance
Jenna Stearns\* 
April 13, 2015 
Abstract
This paper investigates the effects of a large-scale paid maternity leave program on birth outcomes in the United States. In 1978, states with Temporary Disability Insurance (TDI) programs were required to start providing wage replacement benefits to pregnant women, substantially increasing access to antenatal and postnatal paid leave for working mothers. Using natality data, I find that TDI paid maternity leave reduces the share of low birth weight births by 3.2 percent, and the estimated treatment-on-the-treated effect is over 10 percent. It also decreases the likelihood of early term birth by 6.6 percent. Paid maternity leave has particularly large impacts on the children of unmarried and black mothers. 
Keywords: Temporary Disability Insurance; maternity leave; infant health 
1 Introduction
In many countries, maternity leave is thought to be essential to both child and parent well-being. Maternity leave programs are designed to provide compensated and job-protected time off from work so that mothers can prepare for and recover from childbirth and new parents can stay home to care for their infants. The United States is the only industrialized nation in the world without a national policy granting new mothers access to paid time off from work. However, several states offer paid parental leave to qualified workers through state-run insurance programs or state-mandated employer coverage. In this paper, I examine the impact of the introduction of statewide access to paid maternity leave on birth ouomes. Importantly, this leave can be taken in the period before birth. Despite the popular belief that both antenatal and postnatal leave are beneficial to the infant and mother, there is little causal evidence from the United States suggesting that expanding access to antenatal paid leave improves infant outcomes. I find that the availability o statewide paid maternity leave reduces the share of low birth weight births by 0.22 percentage points, or approximately 3.2 percent. 
Paid maternity leave can affect infant birth outcomes through several channels. The availability of paid time off may reduce physical and mental stress during pregnancy. Stress has been shown to have adverse effects on infant endowments of human capital, as measured by birth outcomes such as birth weight and gestational age (Aizer, Stroud, and Buka, 2012; Copper et al., 1996). Maternal prenatal stress is correlated with cognitive development in young children as well (Bergman et al., 2007). Access to paid antenatal leave may be especially important for single or low-educated mothers who are less likely to be able to afford to take unpaid time off and may have more physically demanding jobs. Jobs that involve standing, lifting, long hours, shift work, or noisy environments are all associated with negative birth outcomes (Mozurkewich et al., 2000). Additionally, while the income associated with paid leave may reduce mental stress, there are potentially also direct income effects for some families that could lead to improved birth outcomes. Finally, time off through paid maternity leave may impact choices regarding prenatal care visits or other types of medical care. 
On October 30, 1978, Congress enacted the Pregnancy Discrimination Act (PDA). An amendment to the 1964 Civil Rights Act, the PDA prohibits discrimination in the labor force on the basis of pregnancy. This act requires that employers treat pregnancy and childbirth like any other temporary disability. Consequently, states with Temporary Disability Insurance (TDI) programs were required to start providing maternity leave benefits to pregnant workers. These benefits can be used in the period immediately before an tr birth.This study evaluates the efect o the availability of this paid leavein theve states with TDI programs: California, Hawaii, New Jersey, New York, and Rhode Island. TDI covered approximately 50 percent of all women and over 80 percent of working women in these states in 1978, so access to paid maternity leave expanded substantially under this policy. A 
This paper uses a difference-in-differences (DD) framework to examine the impact of statewide access to short-term paid leave through TDI on the share of low birth weight births in the state. Low birth weight is defined as weighing less than 2500 grams (approximately 5.5 pounds) at birth and is a strong predictor of newborn health and survival probability (Goldenberg and Culhane, 2007). To estimate this effect, I use Vital Statistics natality data from 1972 to 1985. Because these TDI programs all predate 1978, the PDA is plausibly an exogenous shock to the ability of pregnant women to claim disability insurance. This identification strategy allows for the estimation o intent-to-treat (TT)efects  widespread access to paid maternity leave for working mothers. Doing so is possible because workers in non-TDI states do not have any rights to paid leave at the state level.Because it is likely that acess to paid leave has heterogeneous effects across demographic groups, I also estimate separate effects for white/Hispanic, black, married, and unmarried mothers. The results show that access to paid leave reduces the percentage of low birth weight births in a state and that this effect is strongest for unmarried mothers, who are more likely to be employed and thus benefit from TDI. While the ITT effects are modest, analysis of TDI maternity leave take-up in California suggests that the treatment-on-the-treated effects are quite substantial.Furthermore, I demonstrate that TDI increases both gestational age and birth weight at other points in the distribution. Paid leave reduces the proportion of early term births by over 1 percentage point, or 6.6 percent. Additionally, it reduces the share of births in the bottom quintile of the birth weight distribution by 0.39 percentage points, or 2.6 percent. These results are all consistent with the policy increasing antenatal leave taking. Data from the Survey of Income and Program Participation (SIPP) indicate that almost 70 percent of women in TDI states who take paid postnatal maternity leave also take time off before birth during this period Therefore, it is plausible that the policy afects birth outcomes.While this is the first study to evaluate paid leave specifically, the results are in line with research on the effects o other types of leave-taking on birth outcomes in the United States. In a sample of U.S. mothers, Del Bono, Ermisch, and Francesconi (2012) find statistically significant effects of stopping work up to one month before giving birth on birth weight. However, they do not distinguish between different reasons for the work absence or whether the leave is paid or unpaid. Rossin (2011) shows that the availability of unpaid maternity leave in the U.S. increases birth weight and decreases the likelihood of premature birth, primarily for college-educated and married mothers. However, in contrast to studies of unpaid leave, I show that access to short-term paid maternity leave has substantial impacts on the children of unmarried and black mothers. 
This paper is organized as follows: Section 2 provides background on maternity leave through TDI and other family leave programs in the United States. Section 3 reviews the relevant literature, and section 4 describes the data used to evaluate the effects of this policy. The fifth section introduces the empirical strategy, and results are discussed in section 6.Section 7 contains a discussion of possible mechanisms and section 8 presents placebo tests. 5 Empirical Strategy
To identify the impact of statewide paid maternity leave coverage on birth outcomes, I employ a difference-in-differences framework. I compare birth outcomes within states before and after the imple-
$$
Y _ { s m y } = \alpha + \beta T r e a t P o s t _ { s m y } + W _ { s m y } \phi + \delta _ { s } + \gamma _ { m y } + \varepsilon _ { s m y }
$$
The main outcome, $Y$ , is the share of low birth weight (less than 2500 grams) babies born in state $s$ ,month $m$ , and year $y$ . The variable Post is an indicator for month-years November 1978 and later, and Treat is an indicator for the five states that have TDI programs. The variable TreatPost is an indicator for statemonth-years in which maternity leave is covered under TDI. State $\left( \delta _ { s } \right)$ and month-year $( \gamma _ { m y } )$ fixed effects are included as well. There is no need to control for Treat or Post explicitly in the model because these variables are subsumed into the state and month-year fixed effects, respectively. Control states consist of the non-TDI states included in the sample, which is defined below. The vector $W$ contains state-monthyear specific variables including the percentage of mothers in each age and race category, the percentage of married mothers, an indicator for whether or not there is a state law giving women the right to jobprotected unpaid leave for childbirth, and the minimum wag.The data on state unpaid leave is taken from Waldfogel (1998) and the minimum wage data comes from Meer and West (2013). The vector $W$ also includes the yearly female employment rate and labor market participation rate, per capita income, and the share of private sector workers.10 
The errors in (1) are likely correlated within states. When the number of clusters is small, clusterrobust standard errors can be significantly underestimated, leading to incorrect inference. I therefore use the two stage approach suggested by Donald and Lang (2007) as an alternative to clustering to account for group correlation in theerror structure.First, theregression-adjusted differences in the shareof lowrh weight births between the treatment group and the control group are estimated in each period. I include the same control variables as in (1). This yields 168 differences, one for each month-year in the sample. In the second stage, the regression-adjusted differences are regressed on the $P o s t _ { m y }$ indicator: 
$$
\hat { \pi } _ { m y } = \rho _ { 0 } + \rho _ { 1 } P o s t _ { m y } + u _ { m y }
$$
where $\hat { \pi } _ { m y }$ are the estimated differences from the first stage. The DD estimate of the effect of TDI maternity leave coverage on the share of low birth weight births is $\rho _ { 1 }$ . This second stage regression contains only 168 observations and two estimated coefficients, and the errors are no longer correlated within states. 
This method results in correct inference under the assumption that the estimated differences between the low birth weight rate in the TDI states and the control states are subject to independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) shocks. However, if $u _ { m y }$ is not i.i.d., the standard errors may still be underestimated. If birth outcomes in one state are correlated with outcomes in recent months due to state-specific unobserved factors (e.. fu season), then the i.i.d.assumption does not hold.To account for this, I follow Beard and Kuhn (2015) and estimate Newey-West autocorrelation-consistent standard errors for the time series regression in (). This allows unobserved state-specifc shocks to persist across months. In these estimates, denoted DL-NW, I allow for autocorrelation of up to six months.11 
The difference-in-differences estimate, $\rho _ { 1 }$ , is an intent-to-treat effect because the percentage of women who take maternity leave is not observed If this coefficient is negative, it means that the implementation of statewide paid maternity leave reduces the incidence of low birth weight births. It is important to note that some employers offer paid leave to pregnant mothers. However, the vast majority do not. In 2007, the first year for which data is available, only eight percent of U.S. private sector workers had access to paid family leave (including maternity leave) through their employer (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2007). It is likely that a similar or smaller share of workers had access to paid family leave benefits in the time period studied here. While this percentage is small, it is not zero. Furthermore, the PDA likely increased the share of employers who provide paid maternity leave. This does not affect the DD estimate as long as the PDA did not impact employer-provided paid leave differentially across states. Even if it did, however, $\rho _ { 1 }$ is likely biased toward zero. Because individuals are not eligible for TDI if they receive paid time off from another source, the availability of TDI may reduce employer-provided paid maternity leave relative to other states. In other words, the employer-provided leave increase in TDI states should be smaller than it otherwise would have been. X 
Because TDI was enacted in each of these states well before the Pregnancy Discrimination Act came into existence, the implementation of TDI maternity leave is arguably not contemporaneously correlated with other omitted variables that would affect the percentage of low birth weight babies in the state.12 Wage replacement for maternity leave was not, for example, implemented as part of a broader plan to reduce negative birth outcomes. However, the states with TDI worker protection are likely different in many ways from the states without TDI. Identification is threatened if the pre-1979 low birth weight trends are different in the TDI and control states, even after controlling for the variables in (1). Low birth weight trends differ substantially in different parts of the country during the 1972-1985 period, and these trends are often non-linear. This means that the assumptions necessary for DD analysis are unlikely to hold when comparing TDI states to the rest of the country even when controlling for state-specific linear trends; Louisiana and Wyoming may just not be good counterfactuals for California and New York. Furthermore, there are substantial reductions in the power of the hypothesis test to detect treatment effects when the number of treatment groups is small relative to the number of control groups (Conley and Tabor, 2011). There is little reason to think that the five TDI states should be compared to all of the other states. 
In order to select a better contro group or each treatment state, I form syntheticcontrol groups sng Abadie, Diamond, and Hainmueller's (2010) synthetic control method. This method constructs an optimal control group as a weighted average of all the possible control groups based on a set of specified matching characteristis. I create  separate syntheticontrol group for each treatment state, and present results or the five TDI states separately. To form the synthetic control groups, I match on the statewide percntage of low birth weight births, percentage of women in each age group, percentage of married women, and percentage of women in each race group in all of the month-years prior to the introduction of the PDA in November 1978.13 The optimal control group is chosen such that the difference between the vector of predictors for the treatment state and the vector of predictors for the weighted average of control states is minimized over the pre-policy period, and the synthetic control weights for each treatment state sum to one. This method estimates the counterfactual for each treatment state by assuming that the treatment state would continue tofollow the same trend as the syntheiccontrol staten the absenceo TDI materiy leavecoverage.Figure 1 shows the trend in the share of low birth weight births in each TDI state compared to its synthetic control group, as well as for all five TDI states combined. 
This assumption means that the implementation of TDI paid leave must be uncorrelated with other time-varying determinants of the share of low birth weight births, such as the demographic characteristics of mothers. To evaluate the plausibility of this assumption, I estimate the effect of the policy on thebirth rate (using population data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program on women ad 1-49each state, the harebirths to white and blackmothersand the hare irths ma mothers in the state.Although maternal education is not included in the main analysis due to inconsistent reporting in the birth records, I also estimate the effect of the policy on the share of women with college degrees, the share of women whose highest degree is a high school diploma, and the share of women with no high school degree. Finally, because smoking is highly correlated with negative birth outcomes, I estimate the effect on the per capita number of cigarette packs sold in the state (Orzechowski and Walker, 2012). The introduction of TDI paid maternity leave does not affect any of these outcomes, which alleviates concerns that any results are driven by a correlation between paid leave and differential changesin demographic outcomes. The synthetic control method chooses a control group that is in some sense optimal based on the set of specified matching characteristics. However, the decision of what characteristics to match on is somewhat arbitrary and the synthetic control states used in Panel A of Table 3 are not always the states commonly associated with being similar to the treatment states. To alleviate concerns about this method, Panl Bof Table3 hows the same analysis using the states that border the TDI state as its control group. Usingcontiguous states as controls may be a more intuitivechoice, as it is plausible that there are reional trends in birth weight or that neihboring states may have similar cltural practices that are correlated with birth outcomes. Bordering TDI states are excluded from these control groups, and Alaska, Oregon, and Washington serve as the "border" states for Hawaii. Because the control group is not chosen based on matching pre-period state trends, state-year linear trends are included in the model. Results are similar, thoug slightly smaller in magnitude on average, to those in Panel A, and inference is largely the same f the linear trends are omitted. However, the DD estimate is not statistically significant when controlling for state linear trends in a model comparing the five TDI states to all other states. As the results are robust to state trends when arguably better control groups are chosen (e.g., using contiguous control states), this suggests that birth weight trends vary ubstantially across the country and that using all non-TDI states as controls may bias the treatment efect toward zero.Given the similarity of the results between the syntheic control and contiguous state approaches to choosing a control group, subsequent results and analysis are presented using only the synthetic control group approach. This is the preferred method because it s more likely to satisfy the common trends assumption.17 
Considering that the results in Table 3 are intent-to-treat effects, the effects are quite large. Data from California Disability Insurance Claims indicates that there were approximately 753,120 authorized TDI pregnancy-only first claims between 1979 and 1985. This means that 27 percent of women who gave birth in California during this period took paid time off through TDI. Using this as an estimate of the effect of TDI maternity leave coverage on leave-taking among new mothers in California, I can estimate a treatment-on-the-treated effect. The synthetic control group results suggest that for women who took leave through TDI, the probability of having a low birth weight baby decreased by about 12 percent.18 Data on pregnancy claims in other TDI states do not exist during this period, but the average take-up rate in New Jersey, New York, and Rhode Island from 1985-1989 was 26.7 percent. If one is willing to assume a similar take-up rate in 1979-1985, the treatment-on-the-treated effects would be similar in the other states as well as overall. However, the effects of TDI on birth weight are still small in comparison to other interventions such as smoking cessation. Smoking during pregnancy increases the probability of having a low birth weight birth by 100 percent (Lien and Evans, 2005). This suggests that effective smoking cessation programs would be over eight times more effective at reducing the low birth weight rate than policies that provide paid maternity leave. Additionally, the effects of TDI are small compared to the gaps in birth outcomes by race and marital status. Holding constant marital status and age, black mothers are 76 percent more likely to have a low birth weight birth than are white mothers. Conditional on race and age, the probability of having a low birth weight birth is 46 percent higher form unmarried women compared to their married counterparts. 
Effects on birth outcomes should be driven primarily by antenatal leave take-up. Unfortunately, it is not possible to separate antenatal versus postnatal leave from the TDI administrative records. However, data from the 1996 Survey of Income and Program Participation (SIPP) provides some insight into whether or not working women take antenatal leave. Between 1980 and 1985, only 22 percent of women in TDI states who worked during pregnancy did not stop working before giving birth. The majority61 percentstopped working less than two months before giving birth, with 48 percent stopping one month before birth or less. Compared to working women in non-TDI states, those with access to TDI were 30 percent more likely to stop working one month before birth and 26 percent less likely to not stop working at all before birth. The question in the SIPP that allows for the separation of women who stopped working within a month of birth from those who did not stop working before birth is not asked of women who give birth prior to 1980, so it is not possible to causally attribute these differences to the availability of TDI paid maternity leave. This data does show, however, that many women take time off before giving birth during this time period. Additionally, the large differences between the TDI and non-TDI states suggest that TDI paid maternity leave likely had a significant effect on antenatal leave-taking. Furthermore, the SIPP suggests that most women take leave both before and after birth during this time period. Among women in TDI states who report being on paid maternity leave after giving birth, 69 percent also take antenatal leave and 62 percent report being on paid antenatal maternity leave. Given that 27 percent of women in TDI states file a pregnancy-only claim at any point between 1979 and 1985, the SIPP data suggest that at least 19 percent of all pregnant women should have used TDI for antenatal leave at the end of their pregnancy. 
Table 4 shows evidence of heterogeneous effects by marital status, race, and parity. The effects of paid leave are the largest for unmarried women and black women.19 While the magnitudes of the point estimates are larger, the proportion of low birth weight babies is also more than double for unmarried and black women compared to married and white women. The overall results in column (6) of Table 4 suggest that TDI paid maternity leave reduces the share of low birth weight births by 4.9 percent for black women, 2.2 percent for white women, 6.5 percent for unmarried women, and 3.5 percent for married women. Marital status and race are proxies for socioeconomic status, so these results are consistent with the idea that the policy primarily affects low-SES women. Black women and unmarried women are more likely to be employed, which is necessary to be eligible for TDI. Married and white women, on the other hand, are likely to have higher household incomes and thus potentially more flexibility in the choice of whether or not to work. Even conditional on working, black and single mothers have the most to gain from TDI because they are the least likely to be able to take unpaid time off from work. Apart from any direct income effects, paid leave should not affect the birth outcomes of infants whose mothers would havetaken the same amount of timeoff in the absence of the policy. Finally, Panels E and F suggest that there may be slightly heterogeneous effects by parity, although the differences are small compared to those across race and marital status. TDI paid leave reduces the share of low birth weight births by 3.3 percent among women having their first child, and by 2.3 percent among women with at least one other child. This difference may be explained by several factors. First, higher parity births have better birth outomes on average than first births.This may be due in part to first-time mothers having less information about behaviors that are correlated with birth weight and other birth outcomes. Therefore, frst-time mothers may benefit more from interventions that change behavior during pregnancy. Probably more importantly, pregnant women without existing children are more likely to be employed than pregnant women with young children. Therefore, first-time mothers are more likely to be eligible for TDI paid leave than are others.

7 Potential Mechanisms and Subsequent Effects
The effects of TDI on birth outcomes seem large, but are consistent with other estimated effects of access to leave on the percentage of low birth weight births in the United States. These effects may work throug several channels. The literature on the effects of physical and psychological stress on birth outcomes suggests that reductions in stress levels may be an important mechanism through which paid maternity leave improves birth outcomes. Antenatal leave may directly reduce job-related psychological stress in pregnant women.21 Several studies find that stress in the third trimester negatively affects measures of birth weight as well as cognition, health, and educational attainment later in the infant's life (Aizer, Stroupd, and Buka (2012). Evidence also suggests that stress in the third trimester has the largest effects on gestational length (Mulder et al., 2002). However, other research indicates that psychological stress is more important early in pregnancy (Camacho, 2008). To the extent that knowledge about the ability to take paid leave before or after birth reduces worry over finances, TDI may reduce stress early in the pregnancy as well. Additionally, physical stress, which is much more common in some occupations than others, can be reduced when women stop working in the last few weeks of pregnancy. Physical stress is strain or tension on the body, and can also negatively impact the fetus.As is expected, the literature suggests that physical stressors are most harmful at the end of pregnancy. There are other ways through which TDI maternity leave could affect infant outcomes as well. Time of from work may allow women more time for self-care, and can impact choices regarding the quality and quantity of medical care received. Finally, paid leave may have direct income effects that could impact nutrition and medical care at the end of pregnancy. This is potentially important as evidence suggests nutritional changes affect birth weight the most in the third trimester (Rush et al., 1980; Almond, Hoynes, and Schanzenbach, 2011). 
I cannot test a stress mechanism directly, but this theory is supported by the findings of other work that evaluates the effects of exogenous shocks to psychological stress levels immediately prior to childbirth. Eskenazi et al. As terrorism and natural disasters are much more stressful than work, the magnitudes of my results seem plausible in comparison if one believes that stress is the primary driver of the effects. 
Additionally, physical work stress matters. Several medical studies show that adverse working conditions and the lack of antenatal leave negatively affect birth outcomes. A study of Irish working women shows that women who report at least two negative occupational factors (including shift work, high physical demands, temporary work, high job stress, and $^ { 4 0 + }$ hour work weeks) are 4.65 times more likely to have a low birth weight birth and 5.18 times more likely to have a pre-term birth than those who do not (Niedhammer et al., 2009). Finally, U.S. women who work in their third trimester of pregnancy give birth to infants who weigh 150-400 grams less at birth than the infants of mothers who do not work during their last trimester (Naeye and Peters, 1982). The impact on birth weight is largest for women whose work requires standing.

8 Placebo Tests
In this section, I do a placebo test using the synthetic control method to show that my identification strategy does not falsely identify treatment effects in non-TDI states. For each of the 46 states without TDI programs, I create a synthetic control group using the same selection criteria as above. I then falsely label the state as a treatment state and estimate the placebo treatment effect. Because none of these states actually have TDI programs, the treatment effect estimates should be zero. Table 11 shows these results. I estimate a statistically significant (at the $10 \%$ level or below) negative coefficient only 3 out of 46 times. The number of statistically significant results at both the $5 \%$ and $10 \%$ significance level is consistent with what one would expect from a test of that size. Not only are the vast majority of these placebo treatment effects statistically insignificant, but most are very close to zero as well.Only four of the negative coefficients are larger than two-tenths of a percentage point in magnitude. In contrast, Table 3 shows that the estimated treatment effects for four out of the five TDI states are at least this big and also statistically significant. This suggests that the results in section 6 are caused by the implementation of paid maternity leave under TDI. It also supports the use of the synthetic control method as an estimate of the counterfactual: In most non-TDI states, the low birth weight trends continue to follow those o the synthetic control states for the whole sample period.

9 Conclusion
Leave policies that help working mothers balance family and career have many possible benefits. Paid family leave laws have been shown to positively impact maternal labor force participation rates as well as maternal health and well-being. However, evidence on the effectiveness of maternity leave at improving child outcomes is less conclusive. This study is the first to estimate the impact of state-based access to paid maternity leave on infant outcomes in the United States. I find that the availability of paid leave through TDI reduces the share of low birth weight births by 0.22 percentage points, or about 3.2 percent. These estimates are intent-to-treat effects and maternity leave use is limited to working mothers, suggesting that the treatment-on-the-treated effects are over three times larger.Additionally, female labor force participation rates have increased substantially since the end of the sample period. If paid leave similar to that available through TDI were extended to the rest of the U.S. today, one might expect to see even larger effects on birth outcomes.

TDI primarily impacts infants who experience slightly negative birth outcomes, but does not affect very low birth weight births or premature births. This is important when thinking about the long-term benefits of TDI or other policies that provide paid antenatal maternity leave to pregnant women. These policies will not reduce the huge medical costs associated with very premature births, nor wl they prevent the longterm problems associated with these high-risk births. However, even slightly low birth weight and early term births areassociated with negative long-run outcomes.Several studis find that birth weight affects IQ and other measures of cognitive ability around age 7, even when analysis is restricted to children of normal birt weight (Matte et al, 200; Phua et al., 201.These effects on cognitive ability persist and reult in lower wages in adulthood. Furthermore, additional time in utero up to 39 weeks has significant effects on health that persist throughout the lifetime. In this sense, TDI may have important long-term benefits, even if the policy significantly afects only a relatively small portion of the birth weight distribution. The average maximum cost of providing TDI maternity leave during this period is $\$ 3048$ per claim (2013 dollars). Several existing studies f the costs of low birth weight suggest that the possible benefits outwegh this cost. Almond, Chay, and Lee's (2005) twin fixed effects estimates of the hospital costs of low birth weight suggest that a normal birth weight birth costs $\$ 2646$ less than a 1500-2500 gram low birth weight birth. Black, Devereux, and Salvanes (2007) find that a one percent increase in birth weight increases fulltime earnings by about 0.1 percent. Using this estimate, my results suggest that the birth weight increase associated with each prevented low birth weight birth leads to a modest $\$ 2700$ increase in lifetime earnings. Together, these two benefits exceed the cost of providing a mother with TDI paid leave, even without accounting for the many other possible benefits of maternity leave for both the mother and child. 
The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) issues guidelines that suggest women in jobs with no greater potential hazards than those of normal daily life can continue working throughout pregnancy. While this paper cannot identify the exact mechanism through which TDI improves birth outcomes, increased take-up of antenatal leave likely plays a large role. It is therefore important to continue to study the causal effect of work and various forms of physical and psychological job-related stress on birth outcomes to determine if these guidelines should be reconsidered. The ACOG also recommends that women in jobs with occupational hazards stop working before the end of their pregnancy. In the absence of paid leave, many women cannot do so. My results suggest that the effects of paid leave are largest for the children of unmarried and black mothers. This is consistent with the idea that these women may be less able to afford to take unpaid time off from work, especially in the period before birth.Children born into low socioeconomic status families already face numerous disadvantages in life. Given the heterogeneous effects I find, providing working women with access to paid maternity leave at the national level may be arelatively easy way to reduce disparities in health at birth and later in lfe Additional work is necessary to determine the extent to which these improved birth outcomes due to TDI maternity leave persist into childhood and beyond.
