Abstract. I present new stylized facts on the market for managers over the twentieth century, utilizing a novel data set on managerial careers. From the 1930s to the mid-1970s, the level of executive pay, inequality among executives across and within firms, and mobility were low. These patterns have reversed since then. I document that an increase in the importance of general managerial human capital can account for this change. The level of general skills, measured by the type of education and occupational mobility of managers, correlate with higher pay and turnover. Other factors may have also contributed to these changes, but the labor market for managers has likely played an important role.

1. Introduction
The rapid rise in executive pay in the United States since the 1970s, particularly relative to the wages earned by other workers, has stimulated an extensive literature on executive compensation (Hall and Liebman 1998, Murphy 1999, Hall and Murphy 2003, Bebchuk and Fried 2003). Over this period, CEOs have also become increasingly mobile across firms (Huson et al. 2001). Surprisingly, the mobility and remuneration of top managers are often studied independently and, even when they are related, the workings of the labor market for executives receive little attention.1 This paper contributes new facts on the mobility, compensation, and careers of top executives over most of the twentieth century, with the aim of integrating the labor market to our understanding of the changes in pay and turnover. This long-run perspective suggests that an evolving market for corporate executives may have played an important role in the development of the compensation and mobility of top managers. 
An understanding of the long-run trends in the market for corporate executives has been hampered by a lack of consistent data (see Bertrand 2009 for a critical review of the literature). Using hand-collected information on the compensation and the biographies of about 1,000 executives from 1936 to 2003, I find that the level of total compensation and managerial turnover have followed a J-shaped pattern over this period. The inequality among top executives, both across and within firms, was relatively stable until the 1970s, but it has increased rapidly since then. 
The educational background and the experience of top managers have also evolved over time. I find that executives were more likely to obtain degrees in a technical discipline prior to the 1960s, but that formal education in business has become more common since then. Moreover, managers used to concentrate their expertise in one area (for example, rising to top leadership through various promotions in the sales sector exclusively) early in the twentieth century, but the diversity in their occupational experience has increased steadily over time. The changes in educational background and in occupational diversity are suggestive of a transformation in the types of managerial skills. I argue that the market for executives has increasingly placed more weight on general human capital, and less on firm-specific managerial skills. This shift may help to account for the changes in compensation and managerial mobility over the long run. 
To illustrate that such a shift in the type of skills could lead to an increase in pay, inequality, and mobility, I present an equilibrium model of the labor market for executives based on Murphy and Zábojnik (2004), Murphy and Zábojnik (2007). As in their framework, managerial ability is comprised of firm-specific and general skills, but only the latter are transferable across firms. I depart from their setup by studying the compensation of not only CEOs but also of other top executives in a model with search frictions. These frictions impede perfect assortative matching between executives and firms and introduce a wedge between a manager's compensation and her productivity within the firm. Specifically, managerial pay depends on the manager's outside option, which is determined by the executive's productivity in new firms. This outside option increases with the generality of managerial skills. 
In this framework, an increase in the importance of general skills leads to higher levels of compensation, higher turnover, and higher pay inequality. As general skills become more important, the manager's outside option increases, leading to higher equilibrium levels of executive pay. Turnover also rises for two reasons. First, higher levels of pay render low productivity firms unprofitable, which choose to stop operations. Second, high productivity firms endowed with low-ability managers choose to fire them because the benefit of hiring new managers on the job market is higher when those managers have higher levels of general skills. Finally, in the model the degree of skill generality determines the sensitivity of executive pay to managerial ability, but it also affects the endogenous allocation of talent across firms. In my setup, the first effect dominates; the distribution of executive pay both across all managers and among top managers within firms therefore widens with the increasing importance of general skills. 
To assess the model's predictions, I use principal components to create an index of the generality of human capital based on the education and occupational experience of managers, and relate it to pay and mobility. The predictions of the theoretical framework are borne out in the data. A one-standard deviation in the index is associated with an $1 1 \%$ increase in the annual level of pay, a $7 \%$ increase in managerial mobility (measured by the number of firms an executive worked for during his career), and an $8 \%$ increase in income inequality across top executives within the firm relative to the mean. 
Two important caveats should be taken into account when interpreting these magnitudes. First, the skill set of executives is obviously endogenous, and these estimates are therefore correlations. Understanding the determinants of the shift in observed managerial skills is important. Although a detailed analysis of the driving forces of such a shift exceeds the scope of the paper, several factors may have increased the demand for general managerial skills over the century. I discuss possible sources for this shift in Section 5. 
Second, the change in the types of managerial skills has been small relative to the large increases in the level of pay, inequality, and mobility. From the late 1930s to the early 2000s, managerial turnover increased by about $3 8 \% ,$ within-firm inequality among top managers rose by $6 5 \%$ , and the average level of total compensation in the sample increased by a staggering $8 7 0 \%$ . Thus, while the implied elasticities of pay, turnover, and inequality with respect to skill generality obtained from the regression analysis are substantial, it is likely that other factors also contributed to the sharp changes in these trends over time. 
This paper contributes to the large literature studying the determinants of executive compensation.² The importance of general managerial skills for the growth in CEO pay in recent years has been discussed both theoretically (Murphy and Zábojnik 2004, Murphy and Zábojnik 2007) and empirically (Custodio et al. 2013). I add to this literature by showing that a shift in the types of managerial skills may have contributed to the trends in compensation and in mobility over most of the twentieth century. Whether other countries have experienced a similar transformation is an interesting open question. Unfortunately, the United States is the only country to have required the disclosure of executive compensation in a systematic way for a long time period; instead, international comparisons are generally based on recent data (see, for example, Fernandes et al. 2013). Thus, this paper provides a unique longrun perspective on the market for corporate executives. 
A growing literature finds that CEO characteristics affect a variety of firm policies and outcomes (see, for example, Bertrand and Schoar 2003, Malmandier et al. 2011, Schoar and Zuo 2016). The novel long-run trends in managerial careers that I put forth show that CEO characteristics have evolved greatly over time. An open question for future research is to study whether these new stylized facts have had consequences for firm outcomes over the twentieth century. 
Executive compensation, turnover, and career paths are complex phenomena, and it is therefore unlikely that a single factor could account for the evolution of these measures over 70 years. In Section $5 ~ \mathrm { I }$ discuss other factors that have probably contributed to the long-run trends. However, the findings of this paper suggest that the labor market has played an important, but relatively ignored, role in this process.
